July 1985 California Bar · Question 2

Constitutional Law · Combined Guide · Answers A & B

Source: July 1985 selected answers, printed pp. 6–12 (PDF pp. 8–14). Historical tests are identified separately from supplemental modern doctrine.

1. Fact pattern

The County Board of Education schedules public high school graduation on Saturday morning, consistent with district custom. Val, the senior class valedictorian, cannot attend because Saturday is her religious Sabbath. She demands rescheduling so she can attend and deliver the traditional address.

The Board rents the high school auditorium to community groups for evening and weekend meetings at modest fees. NFO advocates racial and religious discrimination and applies to hold a major recruiting meeting on April 20. Opponents demand rejection without consideration. The police chief cites intelligence that anti-fascists intend forcibly to remove NFO from the auditorium. Both Val and NFO invoke federal constitutional rights. The Board seeks advice; rejection of NFO is proposed, not expressly final.

2. Issues in order

  1. State action and incorporation.
  2. Val: sincere religious belief, burden on conduct, neutral scheduling, accommodation and governmental interests.
  3. Establishment Clause limits on accommodation.
  4. Equal protection and discriminatory purpose.
  5. Val's speech interest in delivering the address.
  6. NFO: forum classification and access policy.
  7. Content and viewpoint discrimination; permissible time, place and manner restrictions.
  8. Prior restraint and discretionary licensing.
  9. Incitement, other unprotected speech and freedom of association.
  10. Hostile audience, police protection and less restrictive alternatives.
  11. Procedural due process and equal protection.

3. Val — rules, triggers and application

State action. The First Amendment applies to state and local government through the Fourteenth Amendment. The public school Board acts officially; no private-action exception is necessary.

Free Exercise — historical answers. Both answers distinguish protected religious belief from regulable conduct and balance the burden against governmental interests. B analogizes Wisconsin v. Yoder and Sabbath unemployment-benefit cases: Val loses a benefit earned through academic achievement if she observes her Sabbath. The Board relies on uniformity, convenience and tradition; alternative dates weaken its justification. A treats the burden as potentially substantial but leaves more room for the Board's argument. B favors accommodation.

Modern supplement — Free Exercise. Under Employment Division v. Smith, a neutral, generally applicable rule ordinarily need not satisfy strict scrutiny merely because it incidentally burdens religious exercise. Under Lukumi and Fulton, targeting religion, comparable secular exceptions or discretionary exemptions may defeat neutrality or general applicability. Strict scrutiny then requires a compelling interest and narrow tailoring. Sincerity may be examined; theological truth may not. Here Saturday custom appears facially neutral, and no exemptions or anti-religious purpose are stated. Investigate actual scheduling discretion and comparable secular accommodations. Val has a serious practical burden but no automatic federal constitutional entitlement to rescheduling. Distinguish the historical balancing approach from the modern threshold.

Establishment — historical answers. A sees no religious purpose in Saturday scheduling. B considers whether changing dates improperly aids religion, but concludes accommodation can be permissible and suggests Friday to avoid competing Sabbath conflicts. Both reflect the historical purpose/effect/entanglement framework.

Modern supplement — Establishment. Kennedy v. Bremerton displaced Lemon with analysis informed by historical practices and understandings. Government may accommodate religious exercise without establishing religion; coercion, denominational favoritism and the character of the accommodation matter. A neutral alternative graduation date is ordinarily permissible, not necessarily constitutionally required. No compulsory worship is alleged.

Equal protection. A discusses strict scrutiny for suspect classifications or fundamental rights, but favors rational basis because the schedule is facially neutral and discriminatory purpose is not shown. Modern analysis likewise distinguishes disparate impact from intentional discrimination (Washington v. Davis); deliberate religious discrimination or infringement of protected religious exercise warrants demanding review. Tradition and coordination can be legitimate interests under rational basis. Do not infer intent merely from Saturday's unequal impact.

Val's speech. B identifies a weaker speech claim and characterizes scheduling as a time restriction. Modern supplement: distinguish private student expression from school-sponsored or government-controlled expression; the traditional valedictory address alone does not establish a public forum or unrestricted speech entitlement. A neutral schedule is not evidence of viewpoint discrimination. Determine the school's control and whether the restriction actually targets expression.

4. NFO — rules, triggers and application

Forum. B treats the auditorium as a forum opened for public use. Modern supplement: traditional public forums, designated public forums, limited public forums and nonpublic forums require distinct analysis. A school auditorium is not inherently a traditional public forum. The rental policy suggests intentional opening; determine the scope and permitted speakers/topics. In a designated public forum, content-based restrictions generally require strict scrutiny. In limited/nonpublic forums, restrictions must be reasonable in light of purpose and viewpoint neutral. NFO's recruiting meeting appears within the general community-meeting policy.

Viewpoint discrimination. Rejecting NFO because it advocates racial or religious discrimination targets its viewpoint. Offensive advocacy is not automatically unprotected. Even in a limited forum, exclusion based on disagreement with an otherwise eligible group's viewpoint is ordinarily impermissible. Distinguish discriminatory conduct from advocacy of discrimination.

Time, place and manner. A states the content-neutral, narrowly tailored, significant-interest and adequate-alternative-channels test. In a public forum, neutral restrictions need not be the least restrictive means but must not burden substantially more speech than necessary. Capacity limits, neutral rental rules and reasonable security measures are possible. A categorical exclusion motivated by ideology is not a neutral restriction. The meeting occurs outside school hours; the Board's child-protection argument is weaker, as A notes.

Prior restraint and discretion. Both answers object to advance suppression; B separately identifies apparently broad Board discretion. Modern supplement: advance permits are not invariably unconstitutional. Licensing must contain adequate objective standards and applicable procedural safeguards; content-neutral administrative permits differ from censorship schemes. The demand to reject without consideration and absence of stated standards raise concerns, but the record does not establish the actual policy's full terms. Do not assume all advance denial is forbidden.

Incitement. B expressly invokes Brandenburg: advocacy may be prohibited as incitement only when directed to producing imminent lawless action and likely to produce it. A discusses clear and present danger and imminent lawlessness. Apply Brandenburg, not the older test, as controlling. Intelligence concerns opponents' intended violence, not NFO's intent to incite it. No facts show NFO urges imminent illegal conduct. Recruiting and abstract advocacy remain protected.

Other unprotected categories. A mentions fighting words, obscenity, defamation and misleading advertising. None is established here. Modern supplement: true threats and fighting words have distinct elements; offensive political ideas alone do not satisfy them. Do not conflate hostile listeners with incitement by the speaker.

Association. B recognizes protected membership and recruitment despite objectionable beliefs. Government cannot punish mere association with an unpopular group. Distinguish abstract advocacy from intentional participation in unlawful objectives; no such participation is shown. NFO seeks access for expressive association, not immunity for actual unlawful discrimination.

Hostile audience. A emphasizes police protection and B says police should first control the hostile crowd. Suppression because opponents threaten violence risks a heckler's veto. Assess credibility and immediacy of danger, available police protection and neutral security alternatives. Genuine unavoidable imminent danger may justify narrowly tailored intervention; hostility alone does not justify advance exclusion. Modern supplement: security charges cannot vary according to anticipated hostility to a viewpoint (Forsyth County).

Procedural due process. A discusses notice, hearing, review and lack of waiver. Modern supplement: first identify a protected liberty or property interest and actual deprivation; a rental application does not automatically create an entitlement. Evaluate applicable procedures, including the risk of erroneous deprivation, private interest and government burden. First Amendment licensing safeguards are analytically distinct from general procedural due process. No final denial is expressly stated.

Equal protection. B argues selective denial of auditorium access implicates fundamental speech rights and strict scrutiny. Compare similarly situated applicants and identify the actual classification. Viewpoint discrimination is most directly addressed under the First Amendment; rational basis is not a fallback that validates suppression of protected expression.

5. Advice to the Board

Val: consider a neutral alternative date that accommodates Sabbath observance and preserves her earned opportunity. Historical B strongly favors rescheduling; modern Free Exercise analysis depends on neutrality, general applicability and exemptions. Accommodation is not inherently establishment.

NFO: process the application under objective, viewpoint-neutral criteria. Absent evidence of unprotected expression or a constitutionally sufficient safety justification, approve access on the same terms as comparable groups. Coordinate neutral security measures rather than allowing opponents to dictate access.

6. Answer A versus Answer B

A (printed pp. 6–9) emphasizes belief/conduct balancing, rational-basis equal protection, procedural due process, time/place/manner, prior restraint and police protection. B (printed pp. 9–12) develops Yoder and Sabbath-benefit analogies, accommodation versus establishment, Val's speech, public forum, discretionary licensing, Brandenburg, association, equal protection and hostile audience. B's more explicit incitement and forum analysis is particularly useful; A's procedural analysis adds a separate issue. Neither historical answer should be treated as a complete statement of modern law.

7. Exam traps

  • Applying historical religious balancing automatically to every neutral rule.
  • Using Lemon as current controlling law.
  • Equating religious disparate impact with discriminatory purpose.
  • Assuming every government-owned facility is a traditional public forum.
  • Confusing content with viewpoint discrimination, or advocacy with illegal conduct.
  • Attributing opponents' violence to the speaker under Brandenburg.
  • Treating all permits as invalid prior restraints or assuming a hearing entitlement without identifying the protected interest.
  • Assuming the Board has already rejected NFO.
