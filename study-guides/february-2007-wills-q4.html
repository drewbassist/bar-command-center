February 2007 California Bar · Question 4

Wills and Succession · California law · Combined guide for Selected Answers A and B

1. Fact pattern summary

In 2001 Tom executed a valid typewritten witnessed will while married to Wynn. He left Frank $10,000 and Beta Corp shares, left $80,000 to his deceased sister Sue’s issue, and left the residue to Wynn. Tom crossed out $10,000 and wrote $12,000 with “Okay. 2/15/02.” His son Cole was born in 2003. Nephew Matt died in 2004 leaving Lynn and Kim; nephew Norm survived. Tom died in 2005 with $500,000 separate-property cash and 100 Beta shares acquired with marital earnings.

2. Issues tested, in order

(1) Characterization of stock and cash; (2) scope of testamentary power over community property; (3) validity of handwritten alteration and partial revocation; (4) dependent relative revocation; (5) class gift to issue and representation; (6) omitted after-born child and spouse-parent exception; (7) residuary calculation and final distribution.

3–5. Issue spotting, rules to memorize, and application

1. Community property: Beta Corp stock

Spot it: Tom purchased 100 shares using earnings during marriage, although the shares were titled solely in his name.

Rule to memorize: California generally characterizes earnings during marriage and assets purchased with those earnings as community property, absent a valid change of character. Each spouse owns an undivided one-half interest. At death, a testator may devise only the testator’s half of community property.

Application: The 100 shares are community property. Wynn owns 50 shares outright; Tom may devise his 50 shares to Frank. The $1-per-share valuation does not convert this specific stock gift into a cash legacy.

Exam trap: Do not confuse title with source of funds. Distinguish Wynn’s ownership of 50 shares from a gift under the will.

2. Separate property and the residuary clause

Spot it: The question expressly characterizes $500,000 cash as Tom’s separate property; Article 4 gives the residue to Wynn.

Rule to memorize: A testator generally may devise all separate property. A residuary clause disposes of property remaining after valid prior gifts and applicable estate obligations.

Application: From $500,000, deduct $10,000 to Frank and $80,000 to Sue’s issue. Wynn receives $410,000 in cash, plus her own 50 community-property shares.

Exam trap: Do not deduct the full 100 shares from Tom’s separate-property cash; the stock is separately classified community property.

3. Cancellation, interlineation, and attempted $12,000 gift

Spot it: Tom crossed out the typewritten $10,000, inserted handwritten $12,000, and wrote “Okay. 2/15/02.”

Rule to memorize: Physical cancellation can revoke a testamentary provision if accompanied by revocatory intent. A replacement testamentary gift must independently meet applicable will or codicil formalities. California holographic instruments require a signature and material provisions in the testator’s handwriting; extrinsic context may matter.

Application: Both selected answers treat the attempted $12,000 increase as invalid and analyze whether the original $10,000 remains effective under DRR. “Okay” and the date show intent but neither answer finds a valid executed replacement.

Exam trap: Do not assume crossing out $10,000 automatically makes $12,000 valid. Do not treat Answer B’s assertion that a holograph always requires the beneficiary name in handwriting as a categorical California rule.

4. Dependent relative revocation (DRR)

Spot it: Tom replaced $10,000 with $12,000 in one transaction, evidently believing the increased gift effective.

Rule to memorize: DRR may disregard a revocation conditioned on, or dependent upon, a mistaken belief that an alternative testamentary disposition is effective, when doing so better carries out the testator’s probable intent.

Application: The crossed-out $10,000 was intended to be replaced with $12,000, not eliminated altogether. Both selected answers restore the original $10,000 legacy to Frank.

Exam trap: DRR is a doctrine of probable intent, not a way to validate the defective $12,000 interlineation.

5. Class gift to Sue’s issue; representation

Spot it: Sue predeceased Tom. Her children were Norm and Matt; Matt predeceased Tom leaving Lynn and Kim. Article 3 gives $80,000 to Sue’s issue.

Rule to memorize: When a gift to descendants is distributed by representation, identify the first generation with a surviving member, allocate shares to living members and deceased members leaving surviving issue, then subdivide deceased members’ shares at the next generation.

Application: At Sue’s children’s generation, Norm takes one-half ($40,000) and Matt’s branch one-half ($40,000). Lynn and Kim split Matt’s share equally ($20,000 each). Both answers reach this allocation.

Exam trap: Do not divide $80,000 into three equal shares among Norm, Lynn and Kim. The issue are in different generations.

6. Omitted after-born child: Cole

Spot it: Tom executed the will in 2001; Cole was born in 2003 to Tom and Wynn. Cole was not named. Wynn receives the residuary estate.

Rule to memorize: California generally protects an after-born omitted child with an intestate-equivalent share, subject to statutory exceptions. A key exception concerns a will that devises substantially all of the estate to the omitted child’s other parent, who survives and is entitled to take.

Application: Both selected answers conclude that the spouse-parent exception applies because Wynn receives the bulk of the estate under the residuary clause. Cole therefore takes nothing under the omitted-child statute on these facts.

Exam trap: A residuary gift to the spouse is not automatically enough in every case: the statute’s substantially-all threshold and other requirements must be tested. Do not conclude that every after-born child takes an intestate share.

7. Specific stock gift and Wynn’s independent half

Spot it: Article 2 says “my shares in Beta Corp stock” to Frank, although the shares were acquired with marital earnings.

Rule to memorize: A specific devise identifies particular property. A spouse cannot testamentarily transfer the other spouse’s community-property interest.

Application: Frank receives Tom’s 50 shares; Wynn retains her 50 shares. Answer B notes that “my shares” reinforces that Tom intended to devise only his own interest.

Exam trap: Do not confuse an attempted devise of the whole community asset with a valid devise of the testator’s one-half interest.

6. Answer A versus Answer B

Answer A: Leads with classification of community and separate property, then proceeds beneficiary by beneficiary. It emphasizes that the specific stock devise transfers shares, not their $50 value, and applies DRR to preserve the original $10,000 gift. It also discusses the possibility of a widow’s election, but none is needed to reach the selected answers’ result.

Answer B: Organizes its discussion by beneficiary, explains the spouse-parent exception to the omitted-child rule, and identifies cancellation/interlineation and DRR. Its description of California holographic formalities is overrestrictive; memorize the statutory elements rather than its claim that the beneficiary name must invariably be handwritten.

Accuracy note: These are 2007 selected passing answers, not binding authority or a substitute for checking current California statutes. The guide reflects their shared conclusions; questions about the validity of handwritten changes and omitted-child exceptions warrant checking the precise governing statutory language.

7. Exam traps and hidden issues

  • Community-property characterization is based primarily on acquisition source, not title alone.
  • Tom can transfer his 50 shares, not Wynn’s 50 shares.
  • “$1 per share” is a distraction: the gift is of identified shares.
  • A handwritten amendment to an attested will is not automatically an effective holographic codicil.
  • Analyze revocation separately from effectiveness of the attempted replacement.
  • DRR may restore the $10,000 gift without validating $12,000.
  • “Sue’s issue” is a class distribution; apply representation rather than equal division across generations.
  • Apply the spouse-parent exception before awarding Cole an omitted-child share.
  • Compute Wynn’s residue from separate-property cash after the valid cash gifts.
