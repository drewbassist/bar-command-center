July 2010 California Bar · Question 4

Business Associations | Selected Answers A and B | Issue spotting, rules, application, and recall

1. Fact pattern summary

Alfred, Beth, and Charles orally agreed to establish ABC Computers (ABC), a business manufacturing and selling computers.

  • Alfred contributed $100,000 and stated that he wanted to limit his liability to that amount.
  • Beth contributed $50,000 and provided technical expertise. She was responsible for designing computers.
  • Charles contributed no money but provided sales services. The partners agreed that Charles alone would handle computer sales.

ABC became successful, primarily because of Charles's sales performance.

Two disputed transactions followed.

Deco transaction: Without Alfred's or Charles's knowledge or consent, Beth signed a contract on ABC's behalf to sell computers to Deco, Inc., a company owned by Beth's sister. The price was extremely favorable to Deco and unprofitable for ABC. Alfred and Charles subsequently notified Deco that Beth lacked sales authority and refused to perform. Deco sued ABC for breach of contract.

Zeta transaction: Alfred contacted ABC's component supplier, Zeta, Inc., and instructed its president not to accept orders from Charles because Beth handled technical matters. Charles nevertheless ordered expensive components. ABC refused payment, and Zeta sued for breach of contract.

ABC subsequently ceased operations owing creditors more than $500,000. The problem does not specify the partnership's remaining assets or the amount of either disputed contract.

The examination asks:

  1. How should ABC's debt be allocated?
  2. Is Deco likely to succeed against ABC?
  3. Is Zeta likely to succeed against ABC?

2. Issues tested, in order

  1. Formation of a general partnership without written formalities
  2. Alfred's attempted limitation of personal liability
  3. Equal profit and loss sharing despite unequal contributions
  4. Joint and several liability for partnership obligations
  5. Dissolution, winding up, and creditor payment priority
  6. Beth's actual and apparent authority to contract with Deco
  7. Beth's fiduciary duties, self-dealing, and related-party transactions
  8. Deco's knowledge of Beth's authority and possible remedies
  9. Charles's authority to purchase components from Zeta
  10. Alfred's unilateral attempt to restrict another partner's authority
  11. Zeta's notice and the distinction between internal restrictions and external authority

3–5. Issue spotting, rules, and application

1. General partnership formation

Spot it: Three individuals orally agree to operate a computer business for profit, without forming a corporation or limited-liability entity.

Rule to memorize: A general partnership is an association of two or more persons carrying on as co-owners of a business for profit. No written agreement or state filing is required. Partners may contribute money, property, or services.

Application: Alfred supplied capital, Beth supplied capital and technical expertise, and Charles supplied sales services. Their business relationship establishes a general partnership despite the absence of formal organizational documents.

Both selected answers reach this conclusion.

2. Alfred's limited-liability claim

Spot it: Alfred contributes $100,000 and expressly says he wants his liability limited to that amount.

Rule to memorize: General partners are personally liable for partnership obligations. A unilateral statement cannot create limited liability. Liability protection generally requires a legally recognized entity or applicable agreement with creditors.

Application: Alfred's statement expresses his preference but does not establish an LLC, LLP, or limited partnership. He remains a general partner exposed to partnership debts exceeding his contribution.

Both answers reject Alfred's proposed $100,000 liability ceiling.

3. Profit and loss allocation

Spot it: Contributions differ substantially, and Charles contributes services rather than cash. No profit-sharing percentages are specified.

Rule to memorize: Unless otherwise agreed, general partners share profits equally and bear losses in proportion to their profit shares. Unequal capital contributions do not automatically establish unequal profit or loss percentages.

Application: Alfred, Beth, and Charles each bear one-third of the partnership's internal losses, subject to appropriate accounting and contribution rights.

Charles's contribution of services makes him a partner even though he invested no money.

4. Personal liability for partnership debts

Spot it: ABC closes with more than $500,000 owed to creditors.

Rule to memorize: General partners are jointly and severally liable for partnership obligations under modern partnership law. A creditor may seek payment of an enforceable partnership obligation from a partner, subject to applicable collection rules. A partner paying more than the appropriate share may seek contribution.

Application: All three partners face personal liability for valid ABC debts. Equal internal loss sharing does not restrict an outside creditor to collecting only one-third from each.

Both answers recognize personal liability, although they sometimes use imprecise language suggesting creditors must collect equal amounts from each partner.

5. Dissolution and winding up

Spot it: ABC goes out of business with substantial outstanding debt.

Rule to memorize: On winding up, partnership assets are applied to partnership obligations, including qualifying partner-creditor claims. Capital accounts and remaining distributions are settled under partnership accounting rules. Partners must contribute toward a shortfall according to their loss-sharing obligations.

Application: ABC's assets must first satisfy creditor claims. Alfred's $100,000 and Beth's $50,000 are capital contributions, not guaranteed repayments ahead of creditors. Charles does not automatically receive compensation for his services.

The $500,000-plus debt cannot be reduced to an exact dollar allocation because the question does not state the available assets or final accounting balances.

6. Beth's authority to contract with Deco

Spot it: Beth signs a computer sales contract despite an agreement assigning sales exclusively to Charles.

Rule to memorize: Each general partner is an agent of the partnership for its business. A partner's act apparently carrying on ordinary partnership business generally binds the partnership unless the partner lacks authority and the third party knows or has notice of that lack of authority.

Actual authority may be restricted internally. Apparent or statutory partnership authority may nevertheless protect an outside contracting party.

Application: Beth lacks actual authority to conduct sales because that responsibility belongs exclusively to Charles. However, selling computers is ABC's ordinary business, and Beth is a general partner.

Deco can argue that Beth's status and the ordinary nature of the transaction bind ABC. ABC can argue that Deco knew or had notice of Beth's lack of authority.

Selected-answer distinction: Answer A emphasizes the internal sales restriction and questions whether Deco reasonably relied on Beth's authority. Answer B emphasizes the ordinary authority of general partners and concludes that Beth initially bound ABC.

7. Beth's fiduciary duties

Spot it: Beth secretly contracts with a company owned by her sister at a price extremely favorable to that company.

Rule to memorize: Partners owe fiduciary duties of loyalty and care and must discharge their obligations consistently with good faith and fair dealing. The duty of loyalty restricts improper self-dealing, competition, and appropriation of partnership benefits.

Application: Beth's relationship with Deco's owner, the unfavorable pricing, and the absence of disclosure strongly suggest a conflict of interest and potential breach of loyalty.

The familial relationship alone does not establish wrongdoing. The combination of the relationship and disadvantageous contract is what creates the substantial fiduciary-duty issue.

Both answers identify a breach of loyalty. Answer A emphasizes Beth's potential personal responsibility to ABC. Answer B emphasizes the possibility of rescinding the Deco contract.

8. Deco's knowledge and remedies

Spot it: Deco is owned by Beth's sister, and Alfred and Charles notify Deco of Beth's lack of authority after the agreement is signed.

Rule to memorize: A partner's fiduciary breach does not automatically invalidate an otherwise binding contract with a third party. The third party's knowledge of the partner's lack of authority, participation in wrongdoing, and the availability of equitable remedies must be analyzed separately.

Application: The fact that Beth's sister owns Deco supports an argument that Deco may have known about the improper transaction. But the question does not expressly state that Deco knew the internal sales restriction when it contracted.

The later notification from Alfred and Charles does not necessarily retroactively eliminate authority existing when the contract was made.

Answer A leaves room for Deco to enforce the agreement. Answer B concludes that the fiduciary breach permits ABC to rescind.

Important distinction: Answer B's rescission conclusion is more categorical than the facts necessarily justify. For exam purposes, analyze third-party notice and available remedies rather than assuming every fiduciary breach voids the contract.

9. Charles's authority to order components

Spot it: Charles handles sales but orders expensive manufacturing components.

Rule to memorize: A general partner ordinarily has authority to bind the partnership in transactions within the ordinary course of its business, subject to limitations involving actual authority and third-party notice.

Application: Purchasing components is an ordinary activity for a computer manufacturer. Charles is a general partner, even though purchasing components is outside his assigned sales role.

Zeta can argue that Charles's partnership status gave him authority to place the order. ABC can argue that the internal division of responsibilities excluded this purchase.

The crucial complication is Zeta's prior notice from Alfred.

10. Alfred's unilateral restriction

Spot it: Alfred alone tells Zeta not to accept component orders from Charles.

Rule to memorize: Partners generally have equal management rights. Ordinary-course management disagreements are ordinarily resolved by a majority of partners. One partner does not automatically possess unilateral power to revoke another partner's authority.

Application: Alfred did not obtain Beth's agreement or a partnership decision restricting Charles. His unilateral instruction therefore does not necessarily eliminate Charles's authority.

Both answers recognize that Alfred lacks unilateral power to revoke Charles's general partnership authority.

11. Zeta's notice and enforceability

Spot it: Zeta accepts Charles's order after Alfred expressly warns its president against doing so.

Rule to memorize: A partner's ordinary-course transaction generally binds the partnership unless the partner lacks authority and the third party knows or has notice of that lack of authority. Notice of a disagreement is relevant, but does not by itself establish that authority was validly revoked.

Application: Zeta knew Alfred objected to Charles ordering components. This undermines any claim that Zeta was entirely unaware of an authority dispute.

However, Alfred's objection does not establish that Charles actually lacked authority. If Charles retained authority, ABC may be bound notwithstanding Zeta's knowledge of Alfred's objection.

This is the principal disagreement between the selected answers.

6. Exam traps

  • An oral agreement can create a general partnership.
  • Wanting limited liability does not create limited liability.
  • Unequal contributions do not automatically mean unequal profit shares.
  • Equal internal loss sharing is different from joint and several liability to creditors.
  • A service-only partner may still have full personal liability.
  • A partner's internal job assignment does not necessarily eliminate external authority.
  • Actual authority and apparent or statutory partnership authority require separate analysis.
  • A fiduciary breach does not automatically void a third-party contract.
  • A family relationship is evidence relevant to a conflict, not conclusive proof of wrongdoing.
  • Notice received after contract formation does not necessarily invalidate an existing contract.
  • One partner's objection is not necessarily an authorized partnership decision.
  • A third party's notice of an authority dispute is not necessarily notice of an effective authority restriction.
  • Winding up requires payment of creditors before return of partner capital.

Final disposition

Alfred: Personally liable as a general partner. His stated $100,000 ceiling is ineffective.

Beth: Personally liable as a general partner and potentially liable to the partnership for breaching her fiduciary duties in the Deco transaction.

Charles: Personally liable as a general partner despite contributing no capital. His authority to purchase components requires analysis of the internal arrangement and Zeta's notice.

Deco: Has a plausible breach-of-contract claim based on Beth's ordinary-course partnership authority. The result depends on notice, authority, and potential equitable defenses. The selected answers do not agree on the outcome.

Zeta: Has a plausible claim because Alfred could not unilaterally revoke Charles's partnership authority. However, Alfred's warning raises a substantial notice issue. Answer A predicts Zeta loses; Answer B predicts Zeta wins.

Central examination lesson: Partnership law separates three questions: who has authority internally, when the partnership is bound to outsiders, and which partners ultimately bear the financial consequences.
