July 1998 California Bar · Question 5

Business Associations (Corporations) | Combined selected Answers A and B | Issue spotting, rules, applications, distinctions, and active recall

1. Fact pattern summary

Carl owns 30% of Motco, a publicly held manufacturer of prestigious high-priced motorcycles; none of its other 200 shareholders owns over 2%. Motco's most valuable asset is its large international racing team. Biggo, wholly owned by Rich, offers Carl $25 per share, although Motco trades at $14. Carl receives a warning from the former president of one of three companies previously acquired by Rich that Rich looted those companies; the former president had sued, alleging dissipation of assets. Carl asks Rich and a former employee fired for incompetence; both say the companies failed because they could not compete. Carl makes no further investigation.

Carl sells his shares and agrees to arrange the resignation of Motco's nine directors so Biggo nominees can replace them. Motco's new directors unanimously convert its motorcycle plant to moped production, despite negative publicity and lost racing prestige. Rich has a separate company that makes moped parts. Motco's earnings decline substantially. Pat, a 1% shareholder, files a derivative action against Carl, the new directors, and Biggo. The question excludes federal and state securities-law issues.

2. Issues tested, in order

  1. Derivative versus direct shareholder action; standing and corporate injury
  2. Whether Carl's 30% stake constitutes practical control
  3. Control premium versus sale to a suspected corporate looter; inquiry duty
  4. Improper sale of corporate office through director resignations
  5. Controlling shareholder's duties to the corporation and minority shareholders
  6. New directors' duty of care and the business judgment rule
  7. New directors' duty of loyalty and related-party conflict
  8. Biggo's liability as controlling shareholder; possible looting and minority oppression
  9. Remedies, corporate damages, fees, possible rescission, indemnification and veil piercing

3–5. Issue triggers, memorization rules, and application

1. Shareholder derivative action

Spot it: Pat owns only 1%, yet sues over Motco's loss of profits and business value.

Rule: A derivative action enforces a claim belonging to the corporation. Recovery ordinarily goes to the corporation. The shareholder must satisfy applicable derivative procedural requirements, including contemporaneous ownership and demand or an applicable excuse.

Application: Declining earnings and diversion of corporate opportunity injure Motco. Pat may pursue Motco's claim derivatively, but cannot personally collect all Motco's losses. The selected answers assume the derivative action is properly brought; they do not comprehensively analyze demand.

2. Effective control without majority ownership

Spot it: Carl holds 30%, while no other shareholder holds more than 2%, and can procure the entire board's resignation.

Rule: Control can be practical rather than strictly majority ownership; effective power over corporate governance can trigger controlling-shareholder fiduciary duties.

Application: Carl likely controls Motco notwithstanding his minority percentage. Both selected answers treat him as a controlling shareholder.

3. Sale of control to a suspected looter

Spot it: Biggo pays $25 for stock worth $14; Rich has acquired three failed corporations, and Carl receives a specific warning that Rich dissipated their assets.

Rule: A controlling shareholder generally may sell a controlling block for a premium. Where circumstances reasonably indicate the buyer may loot the corporation, the seller must make a reasonable inquiry and cannot knowingly or negligently facilitate looting.

Application: Carl can defend the premium as lawful and point to his conversations with Rich and the fired employee. Pat argues the unusual premium and specific warning demanded more than asking interested or potentially biased people. Both answers find substantial grounds for Carl's liability.

Distinction: The premium is evidence of possible trouble, not an independent ban on premium sales.

4. Sale of corporate office

Spot it: Carl promises to obtain the resignation of all nine Motco directors and the installation of Biggo's nominees.

Rule: Directors' positions are fiduciary offices, not personal property to sell. Coupling a stock sale with delivery of board positions can constitute an improper sale of corporate office, especially where it harms the corporation.

Application: Carl's undertaking goes beyond transfer of his shares. Answer A expressly treats sale of corporate office as a separate breach; Answer B similarly criticizes conditioning the sale on installing the buyer's preferred directors.

Distinction: A legitimate transfer of control can result in board turnover, but that does not license directors to abdicate their duties for consideration.

5. Oppression and the controlling shareholder

Spot it: Carl receives a private premium while other shareholders remain exposed to Rich's conduct.

Rule: A controlling shareholder must not use control to obtain a personal benefit through conduct that wrongfully injures the corporation or minority shareholders.

Application: Pat argues Carl knowingly transferred control in a manner that exposed Motco and its minority holders to harm. Answer B separately discusses Carl's duty not to oppress minority shareholders.

6. New board: duty of care and business judgment

Spot it: The newly installed directors rapidly abandon high-end motorcycles and Motco's valuable racing reputation in favor of mopeds.

Rule: Directors owe a duty of care and generally receive business-judgment protection for informed, good-faith, disinterested decisions in the corporation's interest. The presumption may be overcome by deficient process or conflicts.

Application: A shift to mopeds is not automatically negligent because it performs poorly. But the new board's immediate decision, negative publicity, and benefit to Rich support questions about care, information, independence, and motive. Both answers identify possible duty-of-care breaches; Answer B explicitly analyzes business judgment.

7. New board: duty of loyalty

Spot it: Rich owns another business manufacturing parts for mopeds; the Motco switch benefits that separate business.

Rule: Directors owe undivided loyalty to Motco and cannot use corporate decision-making to benefit themselves or an interested controller at Motco's expense without satisfying applicable conflict-of-interest standards.

Application: The new board may have favored Rich's moped-parts company instead of Motco. Answer A concludes there is a loyalty breach; Answer B cautions that the directors' actual motives and interests must be established.

Distinction: Separate duty-of-care defects (uninformed decision) from duty-of-loyalty defects (conflicted purpose).

8. Biggo's controlling-shareholder liability

Spot it: Biggo buys Carl's control block, installs the board, and Motco's new direction benefits Rich's other company.

Rule: A controlling shareholder may be liable for abusing control, participating in diversion of corporate value, or oppressing minority holders. Veil piercing to impose Biggo's liability on Rich personally requires additional proof and is not automatic.

Application: Biggo may face liability if it orchestrated the disloyal shift and Motco suffered injury. Answer A emphasizes oppression; Answer B discusses looting and possible piercing of Biggo's corporate veil to reach Rich, contingent on further facts.

9. Remedies and director indemnification

Spot it: Pat brings a derivative action seeking relief for Motco's falling profits and diverted benefits.

Rule: Derivative recovery ordinarily belongs to Motco. Available remedies may include damages, restitution of improper gains, and appropriate equitable relief. Reasonable derivative-action fees may be available under applicable rules. Director indemnification depends on governing law and the nature of the misconduct.

Application: Both answers contemplate corporate damages and Pat's litigation costs. Answer B additionally raises rescission of Carl's sale and limitations on indemnification for directors found liable. Rescission and veil piercing are not automatic remedies.
