July 2006 · Question 5 — Professional Responsibility

Combined guide to selected Answers A and B · Beginner level · California and ABA

Read this first: The question tests the lawyer's professional duties, not whether Client should win her personal-injury case. Discuss each event separately, identify the rule, apply the exact fact, and compare ABA and California when they differ. The published 2006 answers discuss historical California rules; current California rules have since changed in important respects.

1. Accurate fact pattern

Lawyer represents Client, who was seriously injured by Driver's truck. They have a valid written one-third contingency fee agreement. Because Client is indigent, Lawyer orally agrees to advance litigation expenses and lend $1,000 monthly for living expenses, to be repaid from settlement. Without telling Client, Lawyer writes Physician promising payment of medical bills from recovery. Driver has strong defenses and offers $15,000; Lawyer has advanced $5,000 in litigation and living expenses, and Client owes $5,000 in medical bills. Client is reluctant to settle. Lawyer takes her to dinner; they drink two bottles of wine and then have consensual sex at his apartment. That evening, he persuades her to accept by promising to distribute the recovery less his one-third fee and advances, without paying Physician. He does so the next week.

3. Issues, rules, triggers, and application

A. Contingency fee and expense terms

Rule: Personal-injury contingency fees are generally allowed if required written terms and disclosures are met. Under ABA Model Rule 1.5(c), the writing explains the percentage, expense allocation, and whether expenses are deducted before or after the fee. California has additional statutory disclosure requirements.

Trigger: “valid, written contingency fee agreement,” but a separate oral expense arrangement.

Application: The question expressly calls the one-third agreement valid: do not manufacture an invalid fee. Analyze whether the separate expense arrangements and repayment calculations were adequately documented and explained.

B. Litigation expenses versus living-expense loans

ABA: Model Rule 1.8(e) generally permits advances of court costs and litigation expenses, including certain assistance for indigent clients, but prohibits ordinary living-expense loans outside narrow exceptions not shown here.

California (2006 essay): The selected answers describe more permissive treatment of genuine client loans, but identify the oral, undocumented living-expense loan as a problem. Avoid assuming ABA and California are identical.

Application: Advancing litigation expenses is not inherently misconduct. The $1,000 monthly living-expense loans violate the ABA rule; under the historical California analysis, their oral form and terms present a separate violation.

C. Secret assurance to Physician

Rule: Lawyer must communicate material facts, respect Client's authority, avoid personal-interest conflicts, and not undertake unauthorized obligations affecting Client's proceeds.

Trigger: Lawyer promises Physician full payment without telling Client.

Application: This affects Client's recovery and may create an enforceable obligation or personal exposure for Lawyer. Discuss communication, authority, loyalty, and conflicts. Answer B also flags confidentiality only if Lawyer revealed protected information without authorization; the question does not establish such a disclosure.

D. Competence and initial forecast

Rule: Lawyer must exercise competent professional judgment. Application: A mistaken initial prediction does not itself establish incompetence; Answer A raises a conditional issue only if the forecast reflected inadequate investigation or preparation. Do not overstate this issue.

E. Sexual relations with Client

ABA: Model Rule 1.8(j) prohibits sexual relations with a current client unless a consensual relationship predated the attorney-client relationship.

California historical distinction: The 2006 selected answers take different approaches. Answer A sees likely undue influence and a California violation; Answer B emphasizes that consensual relations were not categorically prohibited under the California rule then in effect. Both require examining the surrounding pressure and professional judgment.

Application: No preexisting relationship is stated, so the ABA violation is clear. The timing, wine, and settlement pressure intensify the loyalty and influence concerns.

Current-law alert: California Rule 1.8.10, effective after this exam, now generally prohibits sexual relations with a current client unless the consensual relationship existed before the attorney-client relationship. Learn the historical distinction to understand the selected answers, but use current law for contemporary bar preparation.

F. Settlement decision, pressure, and conflicts

Rule: Client decides whether to settle after adequate consultation; Lawyer may recommend settlement based on reasonable evaluation, but must not substitute personal interests for Client's judgment.

Application: Driver's strong defenses make a $15,000 settlement potentially sensible. But Lawyer wants to recover his fee and advances; the wine, sex, and last-minute bargain raise questions about informed, voluntary settlement consent. Do not say every recommendation to settle is unethical.

G. Distributing funds without paying Physician

Rule: A lawyer holding funds in which a third party has a recognized interest must safeguard the disputed portion and appropriately resolve the claim; a client's instruction does not automatically extinguish a valid third-party interest. The lawyer must also avoid dishonest conduct.

Application: Lawyer expressly promised Physician payment and later distributed everything to Client without paying her. Analyze whether Physician has an enforceable lien, assignment, or other claim; the promise's legal effect is important. If Physician has a valid interest, Lawyer cannot simply ignore it. Both selected answers criticize Lawyer's conduct toward Physician.

5. Essay-writing order

  1. State the valid contingency fee and discuss any separate expense-term defect.
  2. Separate litigation advances from living-expense loans; compare ABA and California.
  3. Analyze secret Physician letter: communication, authority, loyalty/conflict, conditional confidentiality.
  4. Discuss sex: ABA prohibition; historical California rule; undue influence.
  5. Analyze informed settlement choice and Lawyer's personal financial interests.
  6. Analyze Lawyer's promise to Physician and protection of disputed settlement funds.
  7. Mention competence only conditionally; avoid speculative conclusions.

6. Exam traps

  • Do not invalidate the contingency fee expressly stipulated to be valid.
  • Do not confuse litigation costs with living expenses.
  • Do not assume consent to sex eliminates conflicts or improper influence.
  • Do not conclude the settlement itself was bad merely because it was less than Lawyer's forecast.
  • Do not assume Physician has a lien without analyzing the basis of the claim.
  • Do not treat the historical 2006 California sexual-relations rule as current law.

7. Blind active recall

What is the ABA rule on living-expense loans?

ABA Model Rule 1.8(e) generally forbids financial assistance unrelated to permitted litigation expenses, subject to limited exceptions.

Why does the Physician letter create multiple issues?

It is undisclosed, affects Client's recovery, may exceed Lawyer's authority, and creates possible conflicts or third-party obligations.

Who controls settlement?

Client decides. Lawyer may advise but must communicate material information and avoid pressure or personal-interest conflicts.

Why can Lawyer not automatically disburse all funds to Client?

A legally recognized third-party interest in held funds may require Lawyer to preserve the disputed portion until properly resolved.

What is the historical versus current California distinction on lawyer-client sex?

The 2006 answers discuss a narrower historical prohibition. Current Rule 1.8.10 generally bars sexual relations with current clients absent a preexisting consensual relationship.
