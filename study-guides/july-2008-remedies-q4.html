July 2008 California Bar · Question 4

Remedies / Contracts & Sales · Combined selected Answers A and B

Source: July 2008 California Bar Examination, Question 4 and selected Answers A and B (PDF question p. 36; answers pp. 37 onward). This is a synthesis, not an official grading rubric. Some procedural details in the historical answers are dated; apply current governing rules on an actual exam.

1. Fact pattern

Barry, publisher of Auto Designer’s Digest, contracts April 1 to buy Sally’s rare 1932 Phaeton for $200,000, delivery May 25. Only two survive. He pays a nonrefundable $5,000 entry fee to enter the June 1 Concours, hoping a win will boost circulation and advertising. On May 10 Sally says she accepted $300,000 from an Italian collector and will ship the car abroad within a week.

2. Issue sequence

  1. Can Barry sue before May 25? Anticipatory repudiation.
  2. Can Barry prevent shipment while litigating? TRO and preliminary injunction.
  3. Can Barry obtain the car itself? Specific performance or replevin.
  4. If Barry elects damages, what may he recover for nondelivery, speculative business profits, and the $5,000 entry fee?

3. Rules, triggers, and application

Anticipatory repudiation

Rule: A clear, unequivocal refusal or act demonstrating nonperformance before performance is due permits the aggrieved party to treat the repudiation as a breach and seek relief, subject to applicable UCC rules.

Trigger: May 10 announcement of another sale and imminent overseas shipment, though delivery was due May 25.

Application: Barry can generally act before May 25. Sally’s belief that the car is worth more does not undo their valid written contract. Trap: distinguish mere insecurity from unequivocal repudiation; Answer B discusses performance status as an additional wrinkle.

Temporary restraining order and preliminary injunction

Rule: Interim equitable relief preserves the status quo where irreparable harm threatens; analyze likelihood of success, balance of hardships, inadequacy of legal remedies, and applicable security/bond and procedural requirements.

Trigger: Sally plans to ship an almost irreplaceable car to Italy within a week.

Application: Barry has a strong argument for urgent prohibitory relief against shipment: money may not enable him to acquire a comparable car in time. Sally can retain the car temporarily if she prevails; the hardship balance favors Barry. A bond may be required. Trap: TRO is temporary preservation, not a final award of the car; distinguish notice and hearing requirements from ultimate merits.

Specific performance

Rule: Under UCC §2-716, specific performance may be ordered for unique goods or other proper circumstances. Consider a valid, sufficiently definite contract, inadequate monetary relief, feasibility, plaintiff readiness to perform, and equitable defenses.

Trigger: Only two Phaetons survive, this one is in exceptional condition, and the car show deadline is near.

Application: Uniqueness and inability to cover make specific performance compelling if Sally still can deliver the car and Barry can tender $200,000. Trap: a high resale price does not alone defeat specific performance; if the car has passed to a protected third party, relief becomes more difficult.

Replevin / recovery of identified goods

Rule: Replevin seeks possession of identified personal property; under UCC §2-716(3), a buyer may have a right to replevin for identified goods when reasonable cover efforts fail or would be unavailing, subject to statutory conditions and third-party rights.

Trigger: Barry wants the specific 1932 Phaeton rather than a monetary substitute.

Application: The identified, exceptionally rare car makes replevin plausible if Barry satisfies the requirements. Trap: distinguish obtaining possession of the car from an injunction preventing shipment, and address a good-faith purchaser if title has passed.

Nondelivery damages

Rule: UCC buyer remedies include reasonable cover damages (cover cost minus contract price) or market-price damages (market price at the legally relevant time minus contract price), plus allowable incidental/consequential damages, less expenses saved. Avoid double recovery.

Trigger: Contract price $200,000; Sally says another buyer paid $300,000.

Application: If $300,000 is proved to be the applicable market price, a $100,000 differential may be recoverable. The collector’s offer is evidence, not conclusive proof, of the market price. Trap: do not automatically treat Sally’s claimed value as established market damages.

Expected circulation and advertising revenues

Rule: Consequential damages require foreseeability at contracting, causation, reasonable certainty, and reasonable mitigation.

Trigger: Barry hoped to win first prize, then gain circulation and advertising revenue.

Application: Recovery is unlikely: it depends on winning a competition and then translating the win into profits. Even if Sally knew his purpose, the projected amount is speculative. Trap: foreseeability alone does not establish reasonable certainty.

$5,000 nonrefundable entry fee

Rule: Reasonably foreseeable and causally related expenditures made in reliance on performance may be recoverable, subject to limitations on foreseeability, mitigation, and avoiding double recovery.

Trigger: Barry paid the fee after contracting and cannot get it back.

Application: The fee is a plausible reliance or incidental/consequential loss if Sally knew or had reason to know the contemplated Concours use and Barry cannot reasonably salvage the expenditure. Trap: distinguish an actual, provable expense from speculative future profits.

4. Answer A / Answer B comparison

Both answers recognize anticipatory repudiation, the urgency of provisional injunctive relief, the unique-goods basis for specific performance/replevin, the possibility of a contract-to-market damages differential, and the difficulty proving expected business profits.

Answer A develops equitable prerequisites and separates TRO, preliminary injunction, specific performance, and replevin in detail. Answer B emphasizes UCC Article 2, preserving the status quo, bond, third-party purchaser concerns, and why the entry fee is more concrete than projected profits.

5. Exam traps

  • Repudiation permits action before the contractual delivery date.
  • Interim injunction ≠ final specific performance ≠ replevin.
  • Uniqueness makes damages potentially inadequate, but equitable prerequisites still matter.
  • $300,000 third-party price is not automatically the legally proven market price.
  • Speculative future business profits differ from an actual $5,000 expenditure.
  • Discuss whether the seller can still transfer the car and whether third-party rights intervene.

6. Active recall

  1. What facts constitute unequivocal repudiation?
  2. Why is an immediate TRO appropriate before shipment?
  3. What is the difference between TRO and preliminary injunction?
  4. Why is specific performance plausible for this car?
  5. When does replevin apply to identified goods?
  6. How do you calculate cover versus market damages?
  7. Why are advertising gains likely too speculative?
  8. Why is the $5,000 fee analytically different?
