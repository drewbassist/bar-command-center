February 2012 California Bar · Question 1

Trusts | Selected Answers A and B | Issue spotting, rules, application, and active recall

1. Fact pattern summary

Sam, a widower, created a valid revocable inter vivos trust and named himself trustee, with cousin Tara as successor. Annual income was to be divided equally among Ann, Beth and Carol. After the last of these three children died, remaining assets would pass to Sam’s then-living descendants by representation. Sam also executed a valid pour-over will. Two years later he died. Dave, 25, proved he was Sam’s child, unknown to Sam. For three years Tara paid nearly all income to medically disabled Ann, although Beth and Carol had their own assets. Tara resigned. Beth and Carol seek termination and sue Tara.

2. Issues tested, in order

(1) Dave as a California omitted/unknown child; (2) effect of revocable trust and pour-over will on Dave’s share; (3) trust duration, beneficiaries and premature termination; (4) successor trustee vacancy; (5) mandatory equal distributions, trustee duties, breach and remedies.

3–5. Issue spotting, rules, and application

1. Unknown child / omitted heir

Spot it: Dave was already 25, but Sam had never known of him.

Rule to memorize: California Probate Code § 21622 protects a child living when a testamentary instrument was executed if the parent failed to provide because the parent was unaware of the child’s birth or mistakenly believed the child dead, subject to statutory exceptions. Sections 21620–21623 govern omitted children; the exact statutory route matters.

Application: Dave is not an after-born child. Both selected answers treat him as an unknown omitted child entitled to an intestate-equivalent share. Because Sam was widowed and had four surviving children, that share is one-fourth of the relevant estate, subject to the statutory calculation.

2. Revocable trust and pour-over assets

Spot it: Sam funded a revocable living trust and executed a valid pour-over will.

Rule to memorize: California omitted-child provisions can reach qualifying revocable trust transfers as well as probate dispositions; analyze property subject to the omitted-child statute rather than assuming all assets are probate assets.

Application: Both answers conclude Dave’s omitted-child share can be satisfied from trust assets, notwithstanding the trust’s named annual income beneficiaries. Answer B expressly discusses reduction of the named children’s interests.

3. Termination at death of last named child

Spot it: Ann, Beth and Carol are alive; Beth and Carol want assets now.

Rule to memorize: A trust ordinarily becomes irrevocable upon the settlor’s death, absent a reserved or granted power. Modification or termination by beneficiaries depends on applicable law, consent and material purposes; interests of remainder beneficiaries matter.

Application: The instrument specifies continuation until the last named child dies. Ann and other beneficiaries have not agreed. The descendants’ remainder interests also prevent treating Beth and Carol as the only interested parties. Both answers reject termination.

4. Vacancy in trusteeship

Spot it: Tara resigned and Sam named no further trustee.

Rule to memorize: A trust does not ordinarily fail solely because the office of trustee is vacant. California Probate Code § 15660 provides methods for filling a vacancy, including court appointment when necessary.

Application: Tara’s resignation does not accelerate distribution. Answer B explicitly spots appointment of a replacement trustee; Answer A largely omits this point.

5. Mandatory distributions and impartiality

Spot it: Sam directed annual income in equal shares; Tara paid nearly all to Ann.

Rule to memorize: A trustee must administer according to the trust terms (Probate Code § 16000) and act impartially among beneficiaries (§ 16003), considering their respective interests. A trustee cannot replace a mandatory allocation with a needs-based allocation absent authority.

Application: Ann’s medical hardship explains Tara’s motive but does not authorize ignoring the equal-share directive. Beth and Carol have strong breach claims. Both answers find breach; Answer A gives Tara a possible good-faith argument but ultimately rejects deviation.

6. Remedies

Spot it: Beth and Carol sue Tara after three years of unequal distributions.

Rule to memorize: A trustee who breaches may be liable for losses or required to restore improperly distributed amounts, subject to statutory defenses and causation; Probate Code §§ 16420 and 16440 describe remedies and liability.

Application: Measure the unpaid amounts owed to Beth and Carol, seek appropriate surcharge and accounting, and consider recovery of excess distributions where legally available. Answer B discusses constructive trust; do not assume Ann is automatically personally liable.

6. Answer A versus Answer B

Answer A: Focuses on the will substitute, omitted-child share, termination, and separate duties of care, equal treatment and compliance with trust directions. It considers whether Tara’s compassionate motive could support discretion but concludes her distributions violate express terms. Its description of when the trust became irrevocable should not be memorized as stated: the trust was expressly revocable during Sam’s life and ordinarily became irrevocable at his death.

Answer B: More clearly analyzes the remainder class, the rule that a trust does not fail for want of a trustee, and remedies including surcharge and constructive trust. Its discussion of remainder vesting and the rule against perpetuities is supplementary, not the central reason termination fails.

7. Exam traps

  • Do not confuse a previously existing but unknown child with an after-born child.
  • Do not assume a pour-over will excludes revocable trust property from omitted-child analysis.
  • Revocable when created does not mean freely revocable by beneficiaries after death.
  • Current income beneficiaries are not necessarily the only beneficiaries.
  • Trustee resignation is not automatic trust termination.
  • Needs-based sympathy is not a discretionary support standard when the instrument mandates equal shares.
  • Distinguish duties, resulting financial harm, and the remedy.

8. Active-recall checklist

Recite each answer without looking. These checks do not change BarOS progress.

Bottom line

Dave: Strong claim to an omitted-child share, analyzed as 25%. Beth and Carol: Unlikely to terminate the trust prematurely, but likely to prevail on breach claims against Tara. Tara: Resignation does not extinguish liability or the trust.
