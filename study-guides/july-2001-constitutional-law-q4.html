July 2001 California Bar · Question 4

Constitutional Law · Selected Answers A & B · Combined Study Guide

1. Fact pattern summary

Ada completed nondenominational Bible school and served as an ordained pastor. After graduating, she sought to minister at City’s jail, especially to women inmates. Dan, the jail supervisor, told her that ministers ordained and endorsed by a recognized religious group could serve as jail chaplains and receive non-visiting-hours access. The jail would not ordain Ada because she was a woman, and her own religious group would not ordain women. Although Dan offered her the opportunity to serve as a volunteer, Ada sought official chaplain designation. She sued City in federal court seeking an injunction against the rule, alleging First Amendment and Fourteenth Amendment violations.

2. Issues tested, in order

1. Article III case or controversy: standing, ripeness, mootness and redressability. 2. Whether Ada can assert inmates’ rights (third-party standing). 3. State action by City and jail officials; Eleventh Amendment threshold. 4. Free Exercise Clause: classification favoring recognized/ordained religions; neutrality and general applicability. 5. Prison security interests and narrower alternatives. 6. Establishment Clause: denominational preference, government involvement in deciding which groups count as recognized, and historical Lemon-framework analysis. 7. Equal Protection: religion, sex discrimination (including facial neutrality/disparate impact and intent), and asserted fundamental rights. 8. Scope of injunctive relief and distinction between entitlement to chaplain status and equal access to the process.

3. How to spot the issues

• “Federal court” and request for injunction → justiciability, standing, ripeness, relief. • “City’s jail” and supervisor Dan → governmental conduct; Fourteenth Amendment applies. • “Recognized religious group” and “ordained” → denominational classification and free exercise/establishment. • Ada’s group does not ordain women → potential indirect sex impact; distinguish private religious decision from governmental intent. • “Non-visiting hours” → prison security rationale; evaluate whether exclusion is tailored. • Ada especially wants to minister to women inmates → possible third-party rights, but no automatic standing for inmates. • Volunteer access versus official chaplain designation → identify the exact benefit denied.

4. Rule statements for memorization

STANDING: A plaintiff must show (1) concrete, particularized, actual or imminent injury in fact, (2) traceability to defendant, and (3) likelihood that favorable judicial relief will redress the injury. RIPENESS/MOOTNESS: The dispute must be fit for adjudication and remain live; ongoing denial of a claimed right is ordinarily ripe and not moot. THIRD-PARTY STANDING: Generally a litigant asserts her own rights; exceptions typically require a close relationship and hindrance to the third party’s ability to sue. STATE ACTION: Constitutional constraints apply to governmental conduct; a municipality and its jail personnel acting officially are state actors. FREE EXERCISE: Government cannot target religious practice or discriminate among religious groups without satisfying the applicable demanding constitutional review. Neutral, generally applicable regulations ordinarily receive more deferential review under the doctrine used in the selected answers. ESTABLISHMENT: The government may not favor some religious denominations over others or entangle itself in religious determinations. The 2001 selected answers apply the historical Lemon purpose/effect/entanglement framework; modern doctrine has shifted toward history and tradition, so do not present Lemon as the current controlling test. EQUAL PROTECTION—SEX: Facial sex classifications generally receive intermediate scrutiny: an important governmental objective and means substantially related to that objective, with an exceedingly persuasive justification. Disparate impact without discriminatory purpose does not ordinarily establish intentional sex discrimination. EQUAL PROTECTION—RELIGION: Express religious distinctions and burdens on fundamental religious exercise can trigger demanding review; analyze the underlying Free Exercise issue rather than assuming every incidental burden creates an independent equal-protection violation. INJUNCTION: Relief must address Ada’s injury; the constitutional violation does not necessarily compel City to ordain Ada or grant unqualified access to the jail.

5. Application to Ada and City

Standing: Ada personally requested chaplain privileges and was denied, creating a concrete, ongoing injury traceable to City policy. An injunction against the discriminatory requirement could redress the barrier. The claims are ripe and not moot. Third-party rights: Ada’s concern for women prisoners explains her motivation but does not automatically give her standing to assert every prisoner’s free-exercise right. Selected Answer A focuses on Ada’s own claims; Answer B separately discusses the close relationship/hindrance question. Free Exercise: The rule permits only ordained ministers from recognized groups. Ada’s group does not ordain women, so she cannot qualify even if she is competent and has theological training. The government’s reliance on a selective religious credential is not simply a neutral visitor rule. City can invoke safety, security, and reliable credentialing, but should explain why neutral screening, supervision, or background checks would not meet those needs. Establishment: City gives preferential access to certain recognized groups and may need to decide which religions are sufficiently established. The answers discuss secular purpose, effect, and excessive entanglement under the then-used framework. Both see serious constitutional difficulties in the rule. Equal Protection: The policy is facially framed in terms of recognition and ordination, not male/female status. Its interaction with Ada’s church’s refusal to ordain women is evidence of adverse effect, but government discriminatory intent is not expressly established. Answer A gives more weight to the practical gender effect; Answer B emphasizes the absence of proof of City’s discriminatory purpose. Remedy: The likely relief is an order against the unconstitutional eligibility criterion, with legitimate security restrictions still available. Do not claim the federal court can require a private church to ordain Ada.

6. Selected Answer A versus Answer B

ANSWER A (PDF pages 44–49): Addresses standing, the Eleventh Amendment, state action, Free Exercise, Establishment, and Equal Protection. It stresses the discriminatory impact on non-established religions and on women whose religious organizations do not ordain women. It concludes Ada should obtain relief. ANSWER B (PDF pages 50–58): Opens with a broader justiciability discussion (ripeness, mootness, political question, abstention, standing and third-party standing), then treats state action, Free Exercise, prisoners’ religious rights, Establishment, and Equal Protection. It is more skeptical of a separate gender-equal-protection claim because the City policy is facially religious and discriminatory governmental intent is not shown. SYNTHESIS: Both selected answers identify substantial First Amendment objections to the City’s rule. The strongest independent distinction is between an impermissible governmental religious preference and a sex-discrimination claim dependent on proof of governmental intent.

7. Exam traps and doctrinal distinctions

1. Do not confuse a private church’s ordination policy with the government’s own sex classification. 2. Do not skip standing because Ada requests an injunction rather than damages. 3. Do not equate Ada’s standing with automatic standing for all inmates. 4. The fact that a jail can impose security controls does not make every religious classification constitutional. 5. Do not mechanically use Lemon as the modern controlling Establishment Clause rule; these are July 2001 selected answers, and current doctrine differs. 6. Avoid promising Ada automatic chaplain appointment; the proper remedy may be neutral reconsideration under constitutional criteria. 7. Identify religious favoritism separately under Free Exercise and Establishment, even when the factual discussion overlaps. 8. In sex equal protection, identify the difference between facial classification, discriminatory purpose, and disparate impact.

8. Active-recall checklist

□ State the three standing elements and apply each to Ada. □ Explain why the dispute is ripe and live. □ State the rule for third-party standing and identify its weakness here. □ Explain why City’s policy constitutes state action. □ Explain the difference between neutral credentialing and denominational preference. □ Identify the asserted jail security interest and possible narrower alternatives. □ Explain the Establishment Clause concerns and distinguish the historical 2001 test from modern doctrine. □ State intermediate scrutiny for government sex classifications. □ Explain why a private religious group’s policy alone does not establish City’s discriminatory intent. □ State a properly tailored form of injunctive relief. □ Contrast the gender-equal-protection analyses in Answers A and B.
