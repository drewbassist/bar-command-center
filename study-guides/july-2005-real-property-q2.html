July 2005 California Bar · Question 2

Real Property · Combined A/B learning guide · First exposure

Purpose: Learn the rules through the actual fact pattern and selected answers, then transfer the reasoning to new facts. Do not memorize a model essay verbatim. This guide includes guided questions, rules, factual triggers, a seven-part issue outline, three-day practice, and spaced retrieval.

1 · Fact pattern and parties

A Developer subdivided property into ten lots. The original deeds provided for each owner to pay $600 annually for ten years for subdivision security, with payments designated to security company ASI. The original deeds were recorded. Seller acquired a lot under one of those deeds, then transferred it to Buyer under a later deed omitting the payment clause. ASI transferred its service arrangement to MPI. Buyer refuses to pay; neighboring owner Cora wants the obligation enforced and fears an increased burden or lost security if MPI suspends services.

Key analytical distinction: Whether Buyer is bound by the burden is not identical to whether Cora has standing to enforce its benefit.

2 · Beginner doctrine: learn before applying

Ordinary contract versus land obligation

An ordinary personal promise generally binds its contracting parties. A covenant concerning land may bind later owners if the requirements for the burden to run are satisfied. A later deed's silence does not by itself extinguish a properly created and enforceable earlier obligation.

Real covenant (traditionally damages)

For the burden to run under the traditional common-law framework, examine: writing, intent that successors be bound, touch and concern, horizontal privity, vertical privity, and notice. Analyze the benefit and who may sue separately. State and modern-law qualifications can change these elements.

Equitable servitude (equitable enforcement)

Traditionally examine a written promise or recognized exception, intent, touch and concern, and notice to a successor; traditional horizontal and vertical privity requirements are not necessary in the same way. Equitable relief depends on the plaintiff's enforcement rights and the suitability of the remedy.

Privity and notice

TermMeaningApplication
Horizontal privityRelationship of original covenanting partiesDeveloper's original land conveyance
Vertical privitySuccessor's estate relationship to an original ownerSeller's transfer to Buyer
Touch and concernPromise affects land use, value, or enjoymentSecurity serving the subdivision
Record noticeProper recording in relevant chain of title may charge later buyers with noticeOriginal recorded deed, despite Buyer's later deed omitting the clause

Common subdivision scheme

Reciprocal restrictions may be implied or enforced among lots when a developer established a common plan and the relevant owner had notice. The classic implied reciprocal negative servitude concerns negative land-use restrictions; extending it to affirmative annual payments requires careful analysis, not assumption.

Contract issues

Distinguish assignment of rights from delegation of duties; consider any prohibition, material change in performance, and whether a promised security service was actually supplied. A third-party beneficiary argument requires proof of intended, rather than merely incidental, benefit. These contract questions do not automatically establish Cora's property-law standing.

3 · Seven-step guided first-day essay (90 minutes)

Work through these in order. For each step: read the doctrine, answer the prompt without looking at a model answer, and then compare your reasoning with A and B. The guide teaches before testing; the prompts are not an unseen-rule quiz.

Step 1 · Identify parties and promises · 10 minutes

Rule: A personal contract and an obligation running with land are different. A successor may be bound by a qualifying land covenant even if the successor's deed omits it.

Facts: Developer, Seller, Buyer, Cora, ASI and MPI occupy different roles. Developer's original recorded deed contained the annual fee; Seller's later deed to Buyer did not.

First exercise (one question): Does Buyer's deed omitting the payment provision automatically eliminate the obligation? Why or why not? Answer in two or three sentences before proceeding.

Reasoning check: No automatic elimination. The earlier recorded deed may have created an enforceable obligation that runs with the land. Whether it actually binds Buyer depends on the running-covenant or servitude elements, not merely the later deed's silence.

Step 2 · Does the real-covenant burden run to Buyer? · 20 minutes

Rule: Work element by element: writing; intent to bind successors; touch and concern; horizontal and vertical privity; notice.

Apply: The original deed is written and recorded. Security protects the subdivision and plausibly affects enjoyment or value. Developer's conveyance supports horizontal privity; Seller-to-Buyer conveyance supports vertical privity. Recorded title documents may supply constructive notice. Inspect the deed wording for intent rather than assuming it.

Prompt: Which elements are strongest, which require argument, and what changes if the original deed was never recorded?

Step 3 · Can Cora enforce the benefit? · 15 minutes

Rule: The burden running against Buyer does not alone establish Cora's right to sue. Identify the benefited land or party, whether the benefit was intended to run, and whether reciprocal covenants or intended-beneficiary principles support Cora.

Apply: All ten lots receive security, but ASI is named as payment recipient. Consider whether neighboring owners received enforceable reciprocal benefits and whether Cora's threatened loss gives her an appropriate remedy.

Prompt: Why is Cora's enforcement right a distinct question from Buyer's liability?

Step 4 · Equitable servitude · 15 minutes

Rule: Examine writing/recognized exception, intent, touch and concern, notice, enforcement rights, and appropriate equitable relief; do not automatically import the real-covenant privity requirements.

Apply: Security service and the recorded deed favor enforcement; the obligation is affirmative, and Cora's ability to obtain a prospective payment order requires separate attention.

Prompt: If one traditional privity element fails, why might equitable enforcement remain arguable?

Step 5 · Common subdivision scheme · 10 minutes

Rule: A common plan, reciprocal restrictions, and notice can support implied enforcement among owners. Traditional doctrine is strongest for negative restrictions.

Apply: Ten lots, uniform original deeds, and subdivision security support a common plan. An annual fee is an affirmative obligation, creating a doctrinal limitation.

Prompt: Which facts support a common plan, and why is an affirmative fee different from a prohibition on commercial use?

Step 6 · Buyer's defenses · 10 minutes

Rule: Consider omission from Buyer's deed, record notice, whether the fee touches and concerns land, whether a benefit runs to Cora, and assignment/delegation from ASI to MPI.

Apply: Distinguish actual notice from constructive notice. Ask whether MPI's performance materially differs from ASI's promised security. Do not assume the security company's substitution extinguishes the lot obligation.

Prompt: Rank Buyer's two strongest defenses and give a response to each.

Step 7 · Remedies and conclusion · 10 minutes

Rule: Real-covenant enforcement traditionally supports damages; equitable servitudes may support equitable relief when warranted. The identity of the proper plaintiff and beneficiary matters.

Apply: Separate payment owed to the service provider from relief Cora can request. Cora's fear of having to pay more does not automatically make her liable for Buyer's $600.

Prompt: Write a short conditional conclusion for (a) Buyer being bound, (b) Cora's standing, and (c) available relief.

4 · Combined issue outline for Answers A and B

  1. Creation of written covenant in original recorded deeds
  2. Intent, touch and concern, and running of the burden to Buyer
  3. Horizontal and vertical privity
  4. Actual, inquiry, and constructive/record notice
  5. Running of benefit and Cora's right to enforce
  6. Equitable servitude and equitable remedies
  7. Subdivision common scheme and affirmative-payment limitation
  8. ASI-to-MPI assignment/delegation; possible contract defenses
  9. Third-party beneficiary theories and their limits
  10. Damages, injunction/specific relief, and conditional conclusions

7 · Active-recall checklist

  • Explain why omission from a later deed does not automatically extinguish a prior land covenant.
  • State the traditional real-covenant burden elements.
  • Distinguish horizontal from vertical privity.
  • Explain touch and concern using subdivision security.
  • Explain why recording matters to a successor's notice.
  • Separate the burden on Buyer from Cora's enforcement right.
  • Contrast real-covenant and equitable-servitude remedies.
  • Explain the affirmative-fee limitation on the classic common-scheme doctrine.
  • Distinguish assignment of rights from delegation of duties.
  • Analyze an unrecorded-deed variation without assuming the same outcome.
