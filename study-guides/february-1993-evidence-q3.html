February 1993 California Bar · Question 3

Evidence · Selected Answers A & B · Combined study guide

1. Tom's statement about funeral expenses and Boyd's fault

Facts: Mary's lawyer asks Tom, TruckCo's president, whether he called Mary after the crash, told her it was Boyd's fault, and offered to pay Boyd's funeral expenses.

Rules you should know

Relevance. Evidence is relevant if it has a tendency in reason to prove or disprove a disputed fact of consequence. Tom's attribution of fault to Boyd is relevant to TruckCo's liability.

Personal knowledge and foundation. A witness ordinarily must have personal knowledge of the matter about which the witness testifies. Tom would have personal knowledge of what he himself said to Mary.

Hearsay. An out-of-court statement offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted is hearsay unless an exclusion or exception applies.

Opposing-party statement. A party's own statement, when offered against that party, is generally admissible under the opposing-party-statement doctrine. Statements of an authorized corporate representative may be attributable to the corporation.

Offers to pay expenses. Evidence of offers to pay medical or similar expenses may be excluded when offered to establish liability. Whether funeral expenses fall within the exclusion requires analysis; Answer A treats them as potentially distinguishable from medical expenses, while Answer B emphasizes the policy against admitting humanitarian offers.

Compound questions. A question combining multiple factual propositions can be objectionable because the witness's answer may not identify which proposition is being affirmed or denied.

Leading questions. A question suggesting the desired answer may be objectionable on direct examination, subject to exceptions such as examination of an adverse or hostile witness.

Application

The question combines three assertions: Tom called Mary, said Boyd was at fault, and offered funeral expenses.

The statement about fault is relevant because TruckCo disputes responsibility. If Tom was speaking as TruckCo's president within his authority, the statement may be admissible against TruckCo as an opposing-party statement.

The funeral-expense offer raises a separate policy exclusion. The court should distinguish the offer itself from the accompanying assertion about fault rather than automatically admitting or excluding the entire exchange.

The question is also compound and potentially leading. Answer B particularly emphasizes these objections.

Your original error: You recognized hearsay and corporate responsibility, but did not distinguish the offer from the accompanying statement or analyze the form of the question.

Exam-ready conclusion: Tom's statement concerning fault may be admissible against TruckCo, while the funeral-expense offer raises a separate exclusion issue. The compound and leading form of the question supplies additional grounds for objection.

2. TruckCo's liability insurance on Boyd's truck

Facts: Mary's lawyer asks Tom whether TruckCo had liability insurance covering the truck Boyd was driving at the time of the collision.

Rules you should know

Relevance. Evidence must tend to prove or disprove a consequential fact.

Liability insurance exclusion. Evidence that a person was or was not insured against liability is generally inadmissible to prove negligence or wrongful conduct.

Permissible alternative purposes. Insurance evidence may be admitted for another relevant purpose, including ownership, agency, control, or witness bias, where those matters are genuinely disputed.

Prejudice and limiting instructions. Even when evidence has a permissible purpose, the court may exclude it if its prejudicial effect substantially outweighs its probative value. A limiting instruction may restrict the jury's use of the evidence.

Application

TruckCo contends that Boyd was an independent contractor, not its employee.

Insurance coverage on Boyd's truck could support an inference that TruckCo exercised control over the vehicle or had an employment or agency relationship with Boyd.

That is materially different from using insurance to suggest that TruckCo has sufficient funds to pay a judgment or must have been negligent.

The court should evaluate the insurance evidence's actual probative value on agency or control against the risk of improper use.

Your original answer was substantially on the right track. You recognized the exclusion and the ownership exception. The principal additions are agency, control, and balancing.

Exam-ready conclusion: The insurance evidence is inadmissible to establish negligence merely because TruckCo was insured, but may be admitted for the disputed issues of agency, ownership, or control, subject to the court's balancing analysis.

3. Reno's police report containing Boyd's statement

Facts: Officer Reno testified that Boyd told him that he fell asleep at the wheel and crossed the center line. Reno included the statement in his report. Boyd died several hours after the collision.

This is the most doctrine-intensive paragraph.

Rules you should know

Hearsay. An out-of-court statement offered for its truth is generally inadmissible unless an exclusion or exception applies.

Multiple hearsay. When an out-of-court statement contains another out-of-court statement, each layer must independently satisfy an applicable exclusion or exception.

Here the two layers are:

  • Reno's report asserting that Boyd made the statement.
  • Boyd's assertion that he fell asleep and crossed the center line.

Business-records exception. A qualifying record made in the regular course of business, at or near the event, by a person with the requisite knowledge or information, may be admissible if the foundational requirements and trustworthiness standards are satisfied.

Public or official records. A record made by a public employee pursuant to official duties may qualify under an official-records exception if its requirements are met. The officer's lack of firsthand observation of the accident is relevant to the reliability and admissibility of the underlying information.

Past recollection recorded. A record may be read into evidence when the witness once had knowledge, made or adopted an accurate record while the matter was fresh, and now lacks sufficient recollection to testify fully and accurately. Answer A raises this theory for Reno's report.

Opposing-party statement through an agent. Boyd's statement may be attributable to TruckCo if the required agency relationship, subject matter, and timing are established. TruckCo's dispute about whether Boyd was an employee or independent contractor makes this issue important.

Declaration against interest. A statement may qualify when the declarant is unavailable and the statement was sufficiently contrary to the declarant's pecuniary, proprietary, or other qualifying interest when made that a reasonable person would not ordinarily have made it unless believing it true.

Excited utterance. A statement concerning a startling event may qualify when made while the declarant remains under the stress of excitement caused by that event.

Present sense impression. A statement describing or explaining an event while perceiving it, or immediately afterward under an applicable formulation, may qualify. Answer A considers this theory but notes the possible time lapse.

Application

First layer: Reno's report. Reno prepared the report after the accident in the course of his police duties. That supports a records exception, subject to foundation and trustworthiness. Alternatively, if Reno testifies but cannot sufficiently remember the interview, past recollection recorded may become relevant.

Second layer: Boyd's statement. Boyd's admission that he fell asleep and crossed the center line tends to establish his negligence.

There are three important alternative routes:

  1. Agent's opposing-party statement: If Boyd was TruckCo's employee or agent and the statement satisfies the attribution requirements, it may be offered against TruckCo.
  2. Declaration against interest: Boyd is unavailable because he died, and his statement exposed him to potential negligence liability.
  3. Excited utterance: The collision was startling, Boyd was severely injured, and the statement was made shortly afterward. The issue is whether he remained under the stress of the accident rather than having an opportunity for reflective narration.

The declaration-against-interest theory does not depend on Boyd's employment status. The excited-utterance theory likewise does not depend on employment.

Your original error: You recognized hearsay and record keeping, but did not separate the two hearsay layers. You also conflated a declaration against interest with a dying declaration.

Exam-ready conclusion: Reno's report is potentially admissible through an appropriate records or recollection doctrine, but Boyd's underlying statement requires an independent hearsay exclusion or exception. Several theories are available, each with distinct elements.

4. Smith's testimony about Hal's reputation

Facts: Mary testifies that Hal was a kind and considerate spouse. TruckCo calls Smith, a longtime neighbor, to testify that Hal had a reputation for quarrelsomeness and physical violence toward Mary.

Rules you should know

Relevance. Evidence concerning the quality of the marital relationship may be relevant to wrongful-death damages, including the claimed loss of companionship.

Character evidence. Evidence of a person's character or character trait is generally inadmissible when offered to prove that the person acted in conformity with that trait on a particular occasion, unless an exception applies.

Character as a material issue. Character evidence may be admissible when the character trait itself is directly relevant to a material issue rather than being used merely to infer conduct.

Reputation testimony. A witness with an adequate foundation may testify concerning a person's reputation in the relevant community when reputation evidence is otherwise admissible.

Impeachment and rebuttal. Evidence may be admitted to contradict or challenge testimony given by another witness, subject to restrictions on collateral matters and other evidentiary limitations.

Balancing. The court may exclude relevant evidence when its prejudicial effect, confusion, or consumption of time substantially outweighs its probative value.

Application

Mary has described Hal as kind and considerate. TruckCo offers Smith's testimony to challenge that description and reduce the claimed loss of companionship.

If the evidence is offered simply to prove that Hal acted violently on a particular occasion because he had a violent character, the propensity restriction is implicated.

But if the evidence bears directly on the quality of Mary's relationship with Hal, it has a nonpropensity relevance to damages. It may also rebut or impeach Mary's favorable testimony.

Smith's long residence near the couple supports a foundation for knowledge of Hal's reputation, although the court must still evaluate the scope and reliability of that knowledge.

Your original answer identified the important damages connection. The missing analysis was the distinction among propensity, character as a material issue, and impeachment.

Exam-ready conclusion: Smith's testimony may be admissible to rebut Mary's description of Hal and address the claimed loss of companionship, but the court should distinguish those purposes from impermissible propensity reasoning and apply appropriate evidentiary limits.
