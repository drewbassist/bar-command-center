February 2008 · Question 5 — Community Property

Combined guide to selected Answers A and B · Bar examination analysis · California law

Analytical framework: For each asset ask: when and where was it acquired, what was its source, did its character change, and how is it divided? Characterization and division are distinct questions. An asset can be community property yet be awarded entirely to one spouse.

1. Fact pattern and chronology

  • 1995: Harvey marries Fiona in State X and abandons her after two months, without divorce.
  • 1997: Harvey falsely tells Wendy he is single and marries her in State X. They orally agree to live on his salary and save hers for emergencies. His earnings go into joint checking; hers into savings in her name alone.
  • 1998: They move to California, replace their joint checking account with a California account, and retain Wendy’s State X savings account.
  • 1999: Harvey inherits $25,000 and deposits it in his own California savings account.
  • 2004: Dan injures Wendy in an automobile accident. Harvey pays her $15,000 medical bills from his inheritance.
  • 2005: Wendy deposits a $50,000 personal injury settlement in her State X savings account.
  • After Fiona dies in 2006, Harvey reveals the truth; Wendy immediately leaves for State X. Her savings now total $100,000. The second marriage is stipulated to have remained void.

2. Governing rules and statutory framework

DoctrineRule to memorize
Community property (CP), Fam. Code §760Generally, property acquired during marriage while domiciled in California is community property unless an exception applies. Earnings are the classic example.
Separate property (SP), §§770–771Property owned before marriage, acquired by gift or inheritance, and earnings after separation are generally separate. Rents, issues and profits of separate property are separate.
Quasi-community property (QCP), §125Property acquired while domiciled elsewhere that would have been CP if acquired while domiciled in California. At dissolution it is generally divided like CP.
Putative spouse / quasi-marital property (QMP), §2251When a marriage is void or voidable and either party believed in good faith it was valid, the court declares the good-faith party a putative spouse and, if division is requested, divides property that would have been CP or QCP had the marriage been valid.
Transmutation, §§850–852A change in property character generally requires a written express declaration made, joined in, consented to or accepted by the adversely affected spouse. A narrow exception covers certain personal gifts of insubstantial value.
Equal division, §2550Generally divide the community estate equally, subject to statutory exceptions.

3. Threshold issues

A. Void marriage and putative spouse

Trigger: Harvey never divorced Fiona, but Wendy believed his representation that he was single. The question stipulates invalidity even after Fiona’s death.

Application: Wendy has a strong claim to putative status from 1997 until discovery of the invalidity and departure. Harvey knew the truth and is not himself a good-faith putative spouse. Nevertheless, Answer B correctly distinguishes his lack of good faith from his ability to receive a share of QMP when the estate is divided. Do not automatically forfeit his share because of misconduct.

Modern-law distinction: Answer A describes a reasonable good-faith belief. Under Ceja v. Rudolph & Sletten, Inc. (2013) 56 Cal.4th 1113, good faith is subjective; objective reasonableness is relevant evidence, not an independent requirement. Do not confuse a ceremonial but invalid marriage with ordinary unmarried cohabitation. Fiona’s possible competing property rights are outside the specific requested allocation and cannot be conclusively determined on these facts.

B. Oral agreement and title

Trigger: The spouses orally agree to spend his earnings and save hers. Her account is solely titled.

Application: This is a budgeting arrangement, not necessarily an agreement to change ownership. Even if intended as a transmutation, it lacks the required express writing. If characterized as a premarital agreement, the writing and signature requirement of §1611 is not met. Do not memorize Answer A’s suggestion that independent counsel is invariably required for every premarital agreement; enforceability under §1615 is more nuanced. Sole title does not by itself convert marital earnings into SP. Saving for emergencies supports a shared economic purpose.

4. Apply the rules to each account

1(a). State X savings — $100,000

Earnings: $50,000 of the balance is attributable to earnings on the stated facts. Earnings acquired in State X before the move would be QCP; earnings acquired while domiciled in California would be CP, regardless of where the bank is located. Both fall within QMP. No valid transmutation is shown. Divide this portion equally: $25,000 each.

Personal injury settlement: Under §780, damages for personal injuries generally are CP if the cause of action arose during marriage, subject to §781 exceptions (including injury by the other spouse and specified periods of separation). Dan injured Wendy during the putative marriage in 2004; the $50,000 recovery is therefore QMP on these facts. Under §2603, community estate personal injury damages are ordinarily assigned to the injured spouse on division unless the interests of justice require another allocation after considering economic circumstances, needs, time elapsed and other facts. If the exception applies, at least half of those damages must go to the injured spouse. The identifiable settlement remains in the account; depositing it alongside earnings does not itself erase its character.

Conclusion: Wendy $75,000 and Harvey $25,000 before any reimbursement or discretionary adjustment. Both selected answers agree.

1(b). California joint checking

Trigger: Harvey’s wages and replacement of the State X joint account. Any traceable State X earnings transferred into the new account would be QCP; California earnings are CP. Both are QMP. Joint title is consistent with shared ownership; it is not the primary reason wages are community assets. Each receives half of the qualifying balance; no dollar amount is given.

1(c). California savings — inheritance

Trigger: $25,000 inheritance, separately deposited, $15,000 withdrawn for medical bills. Inheritance is SP even during marriage. No commingling or valid transmutation is shown. Harvey retains the remaining $10,000 principal (and any traceable separate earnings on it); Wendy has no share merely because some of the inheritance was spent for her benefit.

5. Reimbursement — $15,000 medical bills

Issue: Can Harvey recover separate funds used to pay Wendy’s medical expenses?

Rule: Family Code §914 addresses personal liability for a spouse’s necessaries of life and provides reimbursement when separate property is applied to such a debt and nonexempt community property or the debtor spouse’s nonexempt separate property was available but not applied. Medical care can qualify as a necessary. Distinguish this from §2640 reimbursement for separate contributions to acquisition of community property, which is not the rule for ordinary medical bills.

Application: Harvey paid with identifiable SP in 2004. The emergency savings and joint checking might have contained sufficient qualifying funds then. Answer A conditions reimbursement on availability at the time of payment; this is the careful approach. Answer B argues that the later balance supports an inference of available funds and alternatively invokes the interests-of-justice exception for the later injury recovery. The 2005 settlement cannot itself establish that funds existed in 2004. Analyze application of marital reimbursement principles in the putative relationship rather than assuming every obligation of a valid spouse automatically applies to Harvey.

Conclusion: A substantial reimbursement argument exists, potentially up to $15,000, but the amount and source depend on proof of available funds and the court’s treatment of the putative estate. Alternatively, the court could consider the medical expenditure under §2603; this is discretionary and must respect the injured spouse’s statutory minimum. Do not award duplicate recovery. If the full $15,000 is allocated directly from Wendy’s injury recovery to Harvey, the State X account would yield $60,000 to Wendy and $40,000 to Harvey; reimbursement from general QMP before equal division would produce a different allocation.

7. Exam traps

  • QCP concerns domicile at acquisition; QMP concerns invalid marriage and good faith. They are not synonyms.
  • Do not treat the State X bank location as controlling post-move earnings.
  • Do not confuse classification of injury proceeds with their allocation on dissolution.
  • Do not assume sole title or an oral budget agreement changes property character.
  • Answer B’s historical married-woman title presumption is irrelevant to these modern deposits.
  • Do not infer the 2004 balance from the 2005 settlement without qualification.
  • Do not assume Fiona’s death cured the stipulated void marriage.

8. Bar-level synthesis and counterarguments

Characterization versus distribution

Apply §§760, 770, 125 and 2251 in sequence: identify the acquisition source and domicile, determine hypothetical marital character, then classify qualifying assets as QMP. Only after characterization apply §§2550 and 2603. The entire $100,000 account is QMP on the stated facts, but equal division of the entire account would be erroneous. Wendy's presumptive $75,000 allocation is not a finding that $75,000 was her separate property.

Competing claims and burdens

Wendy's strongest claim is statutory putative-spouse protection, not enforcement of the oral agreement. Harvey can invoke the community source of wages and shared emergency purpose; Wendy must establish a legally effective change of character to defeat that source-based analysis. Harvey's bad faith defeats his own claim to putative status but does not automatically eliminate his distributive share. The question does not establish the extent of any competing rights of Fiona or her estate; avoid adjudicating those rights without facts.

Reimbursement and alternative allocations

Distinguish the §914 reimbursement inquiry (available nonexempt funds at payment) from §2603 discretionary allocation (interests of justice at division). Harvey's strongest evidence is the emergency fund arrangement and his traceable separate expenditure; Wendy's strongest response is that neither account's 2004 balance is specified. Answer B's inference is arguable, not conclusive. If $15,000 is reimbursed from the $50,000 general earnings pool before equal division, Wendy receives $67,500 including her injury recovery and Harvey receives $32,500 including reimbursement. If instead the court assigns $15,000 of the injury proceeds directly to Harvey, the allocations are $60,000 and $40,000 respectively. These are alternative illustrations, not cumulative awards. The $10,000 inheritance remainder is separate from either calculation.

Timed essay architecture (60 minutes)

Spend approximately 10 minutes outlining, 45 minutes writing and 5 minutes checking. Organize under the four calls: threshold putative status and agreement; 1(a) earnings and injury proceeds separately; 1(b) checking; 1(c) inheritance; 2 reimbursement and alternative equitable allocation. Use distinct IRAC paragraphs for each contested doctrine. State conditional conclusions wherever the record lacks necessary facts.

9. Blind active recall

1. Define CP and SP.CP generally means property acquired during marriage while domiciled in California; SP includes premarital property, gifts, inheritance and post-separation earnings.
2. Distinguish QCP and QMP.QCP concerns out-of-state domicile at acquisition; QMP concerns property of an invalid marriage protected by putative spouse doctrine.
3. Why does Wendy qualify but Harvey not?She believed the marriage valid in good faith; he knew he remained married to Fiona.
4. What is required for transmutation?A writing containing an express declaration accepted by the adversely affected spouse, subject to narrow exceptions.
5. Why is Wendy’s settlement not simply SP?Her injury arose during the putative marriage; characterization is QMP, but §2603 generally assigns the proceeds to her.
6. Allocate the three accounts before reimbursement.State X savings: Wendy $75k, Harvey $25k. Joint checking: half each. California savings: Harvey’s remaining $10k principal.
7. What fact controls the reimbursement argument?Whether qualifying funds were available when Harvey paid the medical debt in 2004; later recovery is a separate equitable argument.
8. Transfer: What if Wendy had discovered the invalidity before Dan injured her?Analyze the cutoff of good-faith putative status before the cause of action accrued; the original QMP injury characterization would no longer follow merely from the earlier relationship.
9. Transfer: What if the $50,000 injury recovery had been spent before division?Section 2603 excludes damages already expended or commingled with other community assets to the extent they cannot be traced. Distinguish identifiable remaining proceeds from amounts no longer available for assignment.
10. Reconstruct both reimbursement theories and quantify alternative outcomes.Distinguish §914 availability at payment from §2603 interests of justice at division; identify the missing 2004 balances, calculate the alternative distributions, and avoid double recovery.
