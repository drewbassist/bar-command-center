July 2011 California Bar · Question 3

Contracts | Combined guide to Selected Answers A and B

1. Fact pattern summary

Betty, a physician, treated elderly Alzheimer’s patient Al for years without charging because she thought he was poor. Al said he would give Betty his office building if she cared for him for the rest of his life, explaining he needed assurance immediately. Betty doubted he owned a building, said nothing, completed his examination, and continued her usual free treatment. Two years later, upon learning he did own it, Betty signed an acceptance letter and placed it outside for next-day postal collection. Al died that night, before collection. The letter arrived after his death. Two years of treatment were worth several thousand dollars; the building was worth millions.

Question: Does Betty have an enforceable contract requiring transfer of the office building?

3–5. Issue spotting, rules to memorize, and application

1. Governing law and objective offer

Spot it: Al promises an office building for medical services.

Rule to memorize: Common law governs service and land-transfer agreements. An offer requires objective present intent, definite essential terms, and communication.

Application: Al identifies the building, the services, and duration. His fear and gratitude introduce a gift-versus-bargain argument, but the conditional language supports an offer.

2. Unilateral versus bilateral acceptance

Spot it: “If you will care for me for the rest of my life” and “I need to know now.”

Rule to memorize: A bilateral offer invites a return promise; a unilateral offer invites completed performance. An offer is interpreted in context and may invite either unless clearly restricted.

Application: The request for assurance favors a promise; the condition of lifetime care favors performance. Analyze both paths rather than assuming one.

3. Silence and preexisting conduct

Spot it: Betty says nothing, finishes the exam, dispenses medication, and continues the same free care.

Rule to memorize: Silence ordinarily is not acceptance. Conduct may accept when objectively referable to the offer; mere continuation of identical gratuitous conduct is ambiguous.

Application: Betty had treated Al for free for years. The same behavior does not clearly manifest a new contractual undertaking. Both answers address the ambiguity.

4. Lapse of offer

Spot it: Two years pass and Betty forgets the statement.

Rule to memorize: An offer with no fixed deadline lapses after a reasonable time in context.

Application: Two years is strong evidence of lapse, especially given Al’s request for assurance “now”; lifetime care supplies Betty’s counterargument.

5. Mailbox rule and death

Spot it: Betty leaves a signed, stamped, addressed letter outside her door; Al dies before collection.

Rule to memorize: A properly dispatched acceptance is generally effective upon dispatch if mail is an authorized reasonable means; death terminates an outstanding revocable offer but does not undo a contract already formed.

Application: Answer A treats the letter as not dispatched until carrier pickup, after death. Answer B treats placement at the normal pickup point as dispatch before death. The difference is outcome-relevant; present both arguments.

6. Consideration and past services

Spot it: Al says “pay you back”; Betty never charged and did not believe the building existed.

Rule to memorize: Consideration requires a bargained-for exchange; past services voluntarily rendered ordinarily are not consideration for a later promise. A promise for future care can be consideration if exchanged for Al’s promise.

Application: Her pre-offer services are past consideration. Her subsequent care was not induced by the building promise because she doubted ownership and forgot the offer. Her later written promise could be new consideration if timely accepted.

7. Promissory estoppel

Spot it: Betty gave free care but forgot the promise.

Rule to memorize: Promissory estoppel generally requires a promise, foreseeable reasonable reliance, actual detrimental reliance, and injustice absent enforcement.

Application: Betty cannot readily show that she changed position in reliance on the building; the longstanding gratuitous treatment defeats the causal link.

8. Statute of frauds: land

Spot it: The promised office building is real property; only Betty signed a writing.

Rule to memorize: A contract transferring an interest in land ordinarily requires a sufficient memorandum signed by the party to be charged, or an applicable exception.

Application: Al never signed. Betty’s signed letter is not signed by Al or his estate. Her care is not possession or improvements; the conventional land-contract part-performance exception is difficult to establish.

9. One-year provision

Spot it: Care lasts until Al’s death, which could occur within one year.

Rule to memorize: The one-year statute-of-frauds category generally applies only where performance is impossible within a year from formation.

Application: Al could die within a year, so this category does not independently require a writing.

10. Mental capacity

Spot it: Al has Alzheimer’s disease.

Rule to memorize: A contract is voidable where a party lacks the requisite understanding or ability to act reasonably in relation to the transaction, subject to applicable doctrine. Diagnosis alone does not prove incapacity.

Application: Assess his understanding when he spoke. His coherent offer favors lucidity; his diagnosed disease raises a genuine defense.

11. Undue influence, duress, unconscionability

Spot it: Al is frightened, elderly, dependent on his physician; the building is worth millions.

Rule to memorize: Undue influence requires unfair persuasion in a relationship of dominance or trust; duress requires wrongful pressure; unconscionability examines unfair process and terms.

Application: Doctor–patient dependence warrants scrutiny, but Betty made no threats, solicited no gift, and did not pressure Al. Disproportionate value alone does not establish unconscionability.

12. Remedy and conclusion

Spot it: The building is worth millions; two years of care are worth thousands.

Rule to memorize: Specific performance of land contracts requires an enforceable contract and equitable justification; alternative restitution requires a separate basis.

Application: Strongest conclusion: no enforceable conveyance because of the land statute of frauds, plus lapse/acceptance and reliance problems. Do not equate the value of free services with entitlement to the building.

6. Answer A versus Answer B

Answer A: Emphasizes unilateral-performance analysis, argues the letter was not dispatched until carrier pickup after Al died, finds no bargained-for inducement, and concludes the land statute of frauds blocks enforcement.

Answer B: Emphasizes implied and express bilateral contract theories, treats leaving the letter at the customary pickup point as dispatch before death, but still identifies two-year lapse and the absence of Al’s signature as formidable barriers. It discusses the possibility of capacity, duress, and unconscionability defenses.

Important distinction: The selected answers disagree about when the acceptance letter was dispatched. The bar question invites that disagreement. Neither selected answer establishes a signed land-transfer memorandum by Al. These are selected passing answers, not an official holding.

7. Exam traps

  • Do not treat Betty’s silent continuation of free treatment as unambiguous acceptance.
  • Do not use past services as consideration for a later promise without identifying a valid exception.
  • Distinguish placing a letter at an authorized pickup point from actual delivery to a carrier.
  • Death revokes an outstanding offer, not an already-formed contract.
  • Do not confuse the one-year provision with the independent land-transfer statute of frauds.
  • Betty’s signature does not substitute for the signature of Al, the party against whom enforcement is sought.
  • Alzheimer’s disease raises capacity; it does not automatically establish incapacity.
  • A gross disparity in value is not by itself proof of unconscionability.

8. Active-recall checklist

9. Exam-ready bottom line

Betty has arguments that Al made a definite offer and that her care or letter accepted it. But two years of delay, ambiguous gratuitous performance, lack of reliance, and competing dispatch interpretations make formation uncertain. Even assuming formation, the land-transfer statute of frauds is a decisive obstacle because Al signed no memorandum, and the traditional part-performance exception is unsupported. The better conclusion is that Betty cannot compel conveyance of the office building.
