July 2003 California Bar · Question 4

Torts: Privacy and Defamation | Selected Answers A and B | Combined study guide

Fact pattern summary

Paula, president of a labor union, whispered to Stan, its secretary, in a corner booth of a coffee shop during a highly publicized labor dispute with City and Mayor. She accused Mayor of refusing to approve a new union headquarters because the union would not bribe him. An unknown person secretly recorded the conversation and anonymously sent the tape to radio station KXYZ. KXYZ knew the recording was surreptitious but broadcast it immediately. Paula sued KXYZ for invasion of privacy; Mayor sued Paula and KXYZ for defamation.

Issues tested, in order

  1. First Amendment protection for publishing unlawfully intercepted material on a matter of public concern
  2. Intrusion upon seclusion and the distinction between interception and subsequent publication
  3. Public disclosure of private facts and newsworthiness
  4. False light publicity and actual malice
  5. Appropriation of name or likeness (addressed in Answer B)
  6. Defamation: defamatory factual assertion, identification, publication, falsity, fault and damages
  7. Public-official actual malice; application separately to Paula and KXYZ
  8. Slander per se versus radio-broadcast libel; truth and privileges

Issue spotting, rules, and application

1. Unlawful recording versus lawful publication

Spot it: The recorder acted secretly; KXYZ received the tape anonymously, knew it was secretly recorded, and aired it.

Rule: Under the First Amendment, a publisher who did not participate in unlawful interception may be protected when it lawfully obtains truthful information about a matter of public concern, notwithstanding knowledge that the source acquired it unlawfully.

Application: KXYZ did not make or procure the recording on the stated facts. Alleged mayoral bribery amid a public labor dispute is newsworthy. Both answers emphasize strong protection for KXYZ.

Exam trap: Do not impute the unknown recorder’s intrusion to KXYZ solely because KXYZ broadcast the tape.

2. Intrusion upon seclusion

Spot it: The speakers sat in a corner booth and Paula whispered, but the setting was a coffee shop.

Rule: Intrusion requires an intentional, highly offensive intrusion into another’s private place, solitude, or affairs; the defendant ordinarily must participate in the intrusion.

Application: Paula has an argument for conversational privacy, but KXYZ did not itself record the conversation. A public coffee shop also weakens a reasonable-expectation-of-privacy claim.

Exam trap: Intrusion concerns the means of obtaining information, not merely its later publication.

3. Public disclosure of private facts

Spot it: KXYZ aired the whispered conversation publicly.

Rule: Public disclosure requires widespread publicity of genuinely private facts that would be highly offensive to a reasonable person and are not of legitimate public concern.

Application: Radio broadcast supplies publicity, but the allegation concerns a mayor, municipal approvals and public labor negotiations. Newsworthiness is the central defense.

Exam trap: A public-interest allegation is not necessarily a legally protected private fact merely because it was whispered.

4. False light

Spot it: The broadcast attributed to Paula an accusation that Mayor demanded a payoff.

Rule: False light requires widespread publicity placing the plaintiff in a misleading and highly offensive position. Constitutional fault requirements, including actual malice where applicable, constrain claims involving public affairs.

Application: Paula actually made the recorded statement. There is no indication KXYZ fabricated or materially altered it. Paula may argue that removing it from context creates a false impression, but the claim is weak.

Exam trap: Distinguish an accurately repeated accusation from a materially false implication.

5. Appropriation / misappropriation

Spot it: KXYZ used the conversation involving union leaders in a broadcast that might attract listeners.

Rule: Appropriation ordinarily involves unauthorized commercial exploitation of identity, not ordinary news coverage of a public controversy.

Application: Answer B discusses potential ratings benefits, but newsworthiness and freedom of expression are substantial defenses.

Exam trap: News reporting is not automatically appropriation just because a broadcaster earns advertising revenue.

6. Mayor’s defamation claim against Paula

Spot it: Paula told Stan that Mayor was a crook who opposed union headquarters because the union would not pay him off.

Rule: Defamation generally requires a false, defamatory assertion of fact concerning the plaintiff, publication to a third person, requisite fault and damages. Accusations of bribery or official corruption are potentially defamatory facts, not merely rhetorical insults.

Application: Mayor is identifiable. Stan is a third-party listener, so the whispered remark is published for defamation purposes even without the radio broadcast. The corruption accusation can constitute slander per se.

Exam trap: Publication to one third person suffices for defamation; public broadcasting is not required.

7. Public official and actual malice

Spot it: The target is Mayor, and the subject is official action on a proposed union headquarters.

Rule: A public official suing over conduct in office must prove actual malice by clear and convincing evidence: the defendant knew the factual assertion was false or recklessly disregarded its probable falsity.

Application: Mayor must prove Paula subjectively knew her accusation was false or seriously doubted its truth. The prompt does not establish what Paula knew; a bitter dispute alone does not establish actual malice.

Exam trap: Constitutional actual malice is not anger, hostility, negligence, or a desire to harm.

8. Mayor’s defamation claim against KXYZ

Spot it: KXYZ broadcast the recording knowing it was secretly made.

Rule: A broadcaster that republishes a defamatory assertion can potentially be liable, but a public-official plaintiff must independently establish the broadcaster’s constitutionally required fault.

Application: KXYZ disseminated the accusation widely. Yet knowing a recording was illegally obtained is not the same as knowing the bribery accusation was false. Immediate broadcast may invite inquiry, but is not conclusive proof of reckless disregard.

Exam trap: Keep illegality of acquisition separate from knowledge or serious doubts about the statement’s truth.

9. Truth, damages, and privileges

Spot it: The accusation alleges corruption in Mayor’s official decision; Paula spoke orally, KXYZ broadcast it.

Rule: Substantial truth defeats defamation. Accusations of serious crime or professional misconduct may qualify as slander per se; broadcast defamation is often treated as libel or as actionable without proof of special damages. Qualified privileges and constitutional defenses must be assessed separately.

Application: Mayor could establish defamatory meaning and publication, but success depends especially on falsity and actual malice. Both selected answers analyze presumed damages and constitutional restrictions.

Exam trap: Do not award presumed or punitive damages for speech concerning a public official without applying constitutional fault requirements.

Comparison of selected Answers A and B

Answer A focuses on false light, intrusion, public disclosure and the First Amendment defense, then evaluates Mayor’s defamation claims separately against Paula and KXYZ. Answer B expressly adds appropriation/misappropriation, emphasizes newsworthiness, and separately addresses defamatory meaning, publication, damages and constitutional fault. Both support a weak privacy claim against KXYZ and identify actual malice as a decisive unresolved element of Mayor’s claims.
