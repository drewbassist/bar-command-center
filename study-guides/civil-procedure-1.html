July 1987 California Bar · Question 2

Civil Procedure | Selected Answers A and B | Issue spotting, rules, application, and recall

1. Fact pattern summary

Pat, a resident of State X, was injured in a car accident in State Y involving Dan, a State Y resident. Dan owned a vacation cabin in State X and spent about six weeks there each year. Pat sued Dan in State X Municipal Court for $200,000 in personal injuries, although that court could hear claims only up to $10,000. Dan unsuccessfully challenged personal jurisdiction, defended at trial, and lost a $100,000 judgment. On appeal, he challenged both personal and subject matter jurisdiction; the appellate court treated both objections as waived and affirmed. The judgment became final. Bob, also injured in the accident, then sued Dan and Pat in State X Municipal Court for $10,000. Pat cross-complained against Dan for damage to her car. Bob, Pat, and Dan made summary judgment motions relying on the first judgment. Both State X and State Y treated contributory negligence as a complete defense.

3–5. Issue spotting, rules, and application

1. Personal jurisdiction

Exam question 1

Spot it: Dan is a resident of State Y, owns a cabin in State X, vacations there six weeks annually, and is sued in State X for an accident that occurred in State Y.

The triggers are out-of-state defendant, limited forum contacts, and claim arising outside the forum.

How it's tested: Can State X exercise personal jurisdiction over Dan based on his property ownership and recurring visits, even though the accident was unrelated to those contacts?

Rule:

A court may exercise personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when authorized by law and consistent with due process. The defendant must have sufficient minimum contacts with the forum such that exercising jurisdiction does not offend traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.

  • Specific jurisdiction: The claim must arise out of or relate to the defendant's contacts with the forum.
  • General jurisdiction: The defendant's connections with the forum must be sufficiently substantial to permit jurisdiction over unrelated claims. Under modern law, an individual is ordinarily subject to general jurisdiction where domiciled.
  • Fairness: Consider the burden on the defendant, forum state's interest, plaintiff's interest, and other relevant fairness factors.

Application: Dan's cabin and vacations establish contacts with State X, but the accident occurred in State Y. The selected answers conclude that personal jurisdiction is probably lacking.

Exam trap: Owning property in a state does not automatically establish jurisdiction over an unrelated personal injury lawsuit.

2. Waiver of personal jurisdiction

Exam question 2

Spot it: Dan moves to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction. His motion is denied. He then answers, proceeds to trial, loses, and appeals.

The trigger is a defendant who objects to jurisdiction but subsequently participates in litigation.

How it's tested: Did Dan preserve his personal jurisdiction objection, or waive it by defending the case?

Rule:

Personal jurisdiction is a waivable defense. A defendant must raise the objection in the manner and at the time required by the applicable procedural rules. A timely preserved objection generally is not waived merely by defending on the merits after the objection is rejected.

Application: Dan challenged personal jurisdiction before answering. Answer A treats the timely objection as preserved. Answer B discusses special appearances and whether participation constituted a general appearance.

Exam trap: Don't automatically conclude that defending a lawsuit waives personal jurisdiction after a timely objection.

3. Subject matter jurisdiction and waiver

Exam question 2

Spot it: Dan raises subject matter jurisdiction for the first time on appeal, and the appellate court says he waived it by participating in the trial.

The trigger is a late subject matter jurisdiction objection.

How it's tested: Can a defendant waive a lack of subject matter jurisdiction by failing to object earlier?

Rule:

Subject matter jurisdiction is the court's authority to adjudicate a particular category of dispute. It cannot be created by consent, participation, or waiver. A genuine lack of subject matter jurisdiction may generally be raised at any stage of the proceedings, subject to rules governing final judgments and collateral attacks.

Application: Both selected answers say the appellate court was wrong to treat subject matter jurisdiction as waivable.

Exam trap: Distinguish the power to hear the case from the power to bind the particular defendant.

4. Final judgment and collateral attack

Questions 2–4

Spot it: The appellate court affirmed the $100,000 judgment, and the judgment became final, even though the Municipal Court's jurisdictional ceiling was $10,000.

How it's tested: Does a final judgment have preclusive effect when the original court lacked subject matter jurisdiction?

Rule:

A final judgment ordinarily receives preclusive effect even if it contains legal errors. However, a judgment entered without subject matter jurisdiction may be vulnerable to challenge. The effect of a jurisdictionally defective final judgment depends on the applicable rules governing direct review, collateral attack, and finality.

Application: Answer A questions the judgment's preclusive effect because the Municipal Court exceeded its jurisdiction. Answer B emphasizes that the judgment was affirmed and became final, and analyzes whether the jurisdictional determination can be challenged again.

Exam trap: Don't confuse a judgment being erroneous with a judgment being void. And don't assume that every subject matter jurisdiction defect permits unlimited collateral attacks after final judgment.

5. Summary judgment

Questions 3–6

Spot it: Bob, Pat, and Dan each move for summary judgment based on the first lawsuit.

How it's tested: Has the moving party established that there is no genuine dispute of material fact and that the party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law?

Rule:

Summary judgment is appropriate when there is no genuine dispute of material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.

A party may obtain partial summary judgment or summary adjudication on a particular issue where the applicable procedural rules permit it.

Application: The question expressly asks whether Bob and Pat can establish Dan's negligence through the earlier judgment. It does not necessarily require them to establish all elements of their claims.

This is an important distinction from the full-claim summary judgment analysis.

6. Offensive nonmutual collateral estoppel

Question 3: Bob v. Dan

Spot it: Bob was not a party to the first lawsuit, but he wants to use the finding that Dan was negligent.

How it's tested: Can a new plaintiff use a previous judgment against a defendant who already litigated and lost the same issue?

Rule:

Issue preclusion generally requires:

  1. An identical issue.
  2. Actually litigated.
  3. Necessarily decided.
  4. A final judgment on the merits.
  5. The party being bound was a party to the prior proceeding or in legally sufficient privity.

Nonmutual offensive issue preclusion may be permitted, subject to fairness considerations and applicable jurisdictional rules.

Application: Bob wants to establish Dan's negligence through the first verdict. Answer A raises the jurisdictional defect and fairness concerns. Answer B emphasizes that offensive nonmutual preclusion is discretionary and may be inappropriate if Bob could have joined the first action.

Exam trap: Mutuality is not invariably required. The important question is whether the person being bound, Dan, had a full and fair opportunity to litigate.

7. Same-party issue preclusion

Question 4: Pat v. Dan

Spot it: Pat already litigated Dan's negligence successfully and now asserts a claim for damage to her car.

How it's tested: Can Pat reuse the finding of negligence against the same defendant?

Rule:

A party may be precluded from relitigating an identical issue that was actually litigated, necessarily decided, and embodied in a sufficiently final judgment, when the requirements of fairness and applicable law are satisfied.

Application: Pat and Dan were parties to the original action, so the nonmutual offensive-use problem present in Bob's case does not arise in the same way.

However, the original judgment's jurisdictional defect and the potential claim-preclusion bar must also be analyzed.

Exam trap: A party may satisfy the requirements of issue preclusion but still be barred from bringing the new claim by claim preclusion.

8. Due process and nonparty preclusion

Question 5: Pat v. Bob

Spot it: Pat seeks summary judgment against Bob using a judgment from litigation in which Bob did not participate.

How it's tested: Can Bob be bound by findings in Pat's earlier lawsuit against Dan?

Rule:

Due process generally prohibits binding a nonparty to a prior judgment unless a recognized exception applies, such as legally sufficient privity, representation, or another established nonparty-preclusion relationship.

Application: Bob was a bystander injured in the collision. He was not a party to Pat's first lawsuit, and nothing establishes that he was represented by Pat.

Pat therefore cannot use the first judgment to bind Bob.

Exam trap: A judgment may sometimes benefit a nonparty, but it ordinarily cannot be imposed against a nonparty without a recognized legal basis.

9. Claim preclusion and splitting causes of action

Question 6: Dan v. Pat

Spot it: Pat previously recovered for personal injuries arising from the accident and now brings a cross-complaint for damage to her car arising from that same collision.

How it's tested: Was Pat required to bring both claims in the first lawsuit?

Rule:

Claim preclusion generally bars a subsequent action involving the same claim between the same parties or their privies after a valid final judgment on the merits.

Under the transactional approach, claims arising from the same transaction or occurrence ordinarily must be litigated together.

Under California's primary-rights approach, different injuries to distinct primary rights may constitute separate causes of action even when arising from the same occurrence.

Application: Pat's bodily injuries and damage to her car arose from one accident.

  • Transactional approach: Dan has a strong argument that Pat improperly split her claim.
  • Primary-rights approach: Pat's personal injury and property damage may be treated as distinct causes of action.

Both model answers discuss this distinction.

Exam trap: The fact that two claims arise from the same accident does not necessarily mean they constitute the same cause of action under California's primary-rights doctrine.

10. Contributory negligence and what the first verdict actually decided

Spot it: Dan answered that Pat's negligence caused the accident, and both State X and State Y treat contributory negligence as a complete defense.

How it's tested: What issues were necessarily resolved when the jury returned a verdict for Pat?

Rule:

For an issue to have preclusive effect, it must have been actually litigated and necessarily decided in the prior proceeding.

Application: The jury's verdict for Pat necessarily rejected Dan's contributory negligence defense under the stated law.

But that finding concerns Pat's negligence, not Bob's.

It does not automatically establish whether Bob was negligent or whether Bob's injuries were caused by Dan.

This distinction matters particularly to Pat's attempted use of the judgment against Bob.

6. Exam traps

  • Property ownership and recurring visits do not automatically support general jurisdiction over an unrelated claim.
  • Personal jurisdiction may be waived; genuine subject matter jurisdiction defects cannot be cured by consent.
  • A timely personal-jurisdiction objection is not necessarily lost by subsequent participation.
  • A final judgment with a jurisdictional defect requires separate analysis of direct review, collateral attack, and preclusion.
  • Issue preclusion requires an issue actually litigated and necessarily decided, not merely discussed.
  • Offensive nonmutual preclusion requires careful fairness analysis.
  • A nonparty ordinarily cannot be bound by a judgment without an established exception.
  • Issue preclusion and claim preclusion are different inquiries.
  • California's primary-rights approach can differ from the transactional approach to claim preclusion.
  • A verdict rejecting Pat's contributory negligence does not establish Bob's negligence.
